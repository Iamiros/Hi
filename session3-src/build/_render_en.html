How to Use This Guide

This guide is built to be read once, slowly — and then revisited many times, quickly. Everything you need is inside it; you should not have to open the source textbooks again.

The organizing idea

All six topics in this session are variations on a single question: what happens when blood is in the wrong place, in the wrong amount, or in the wrong physical state? Blood can pool where it should have drained (congestion), its watery fraction can leak out of the vessel (edema), the whole of it can escape through a breach in the wall (hemorrhage), it can solidify inside a vessel that is still alive (thrombosis), that solid mass can travel downstream (embolism), and where it finally lodges the tissue beyond it dies (infarction). Read in that order, the five sections form one continuous story rather than six separate lists.

Hyperemia, Congestion
and Edema

Too much blood inside the vessels, and too much water outside them. These two problems are physically different but mechanically linked: the same raised venous pressure that engorges a capillary bed also pushes fluid out of it. Understanding that link is the single most useful thing in this Part.

By the end of this Part you should be able to

  • Distinguish active hyperemia from passive congestion by mechanism, colour, temperature and clinical setting.
  • List and explain at least three morphological features each of chronic pulmonary congestion and nutmeg liver.
  • Explain, from first principles, why hemosiderin-laden macrophages appear in the alveoli of a patient in left heart failure.
  • Derive all four mechanisms of non-inflammatory edema from the Starling equation.
  • Separate a transudate from an exudate on protein content, specific gravity and cellularity, and say what each implies.

1.1 The Normal Microcirculation

Before you can call something abnormal you need the baseline. A capillary bed sits between an arteriole (inflow, high pressure, controlled by smooth-muscle tone) and a venule (outflow, low pressure, essentially passive). The volume of blood standing in the bed at any moment is simply the balance of those two: volume = what comes in − what goes out.

There are therefore only two ways to increase the local blood volume, and they are mirror images of one another:

  • Open the tap wider — arteriolar dilation increases inflow. This is hyperemia (active).
  • Block the drain — impaired venous outflow traps blood in the bed. This is congestion (passive).

Both are grouped under the umbrella term hyperemia in the classical literature — "a local increase in the volume of blood caused by dilatation of the small vessels" — but in modern usage hyperemia alone means the active arterial form, and the passive form is called congestion. Your examiner will expect the modern usage.

High-Yield

Hyperemia and congestion both increase intravascular volume. Edema increases extravascular (interstitial) volume. Congestion very often causes edema, because the raised venous pressure that produces the congestion is also the force that drives fluid across the capillary wall — but they are not the same thing and are not interchangeable terms.

1.2 Hyperemia — the Active, Arterial Process

Definition

Hyperemia (active hyperemia) is an active increase in local blood volume produced by arteriolar dilation, which augments arterial inflow into a tissue.

The word active is doing real work here. The vessel is not passively filling; a signal has actively relaxed arteriolar smooth muscle. The commonest signals are neurogenic (sympathetic withdrawal, as in blushing), metabolic (adenosine, CO2, H+, K+ accumulating in exercising muscle), and inflammatory (histamine, bradykinin, prostaglandins, nitric oxide at a site of injury).

Mechanism — why hyperemic tissue is red and warm
  1. A vasodilator signal (metabolic, neurogenic or inflammatory) relaxes arteriolar smooth muscle.Arteriolar tone is the main resistance point of the circulation; relaxing it drops downstream resistance more than any other single change.
  2. Resistance falls, so flow through the capillary bed rises sharply.Flow ∝ pressure gradient ÷ resistance; with the gradient unchanged, halving resistance doubles flow.
  3. The bed fills with blood arriving directly from the arterial side, which is fully oxygenated and therefore bright red.Oxyhemoglobin is scarlet; deoxyhemoglobin is dark purple-blue. Colour follows the saturation of the haemoglobin actually present.
  4. Because flow is fast, oxygen extraction per unit volume is low — blood leaves the bed still well oxygenated.Transit time is short, so each red cell gives up less of its oxygen before it exits.
  5. The large volume of warm core blood delivered per minute raises the surface temperature of the tissue.Blood is the body's main convective heat carrier; more flow means more heat delivered to the periphery.
A tissue that is bright red (erythema), warm, and slightly swollen, with increased metabolism and function. Two of the five cardinal signs of inflammation — rubor and calor — are simply active hyperemia.

Typical settings

  • Exercising skeletal muscle — local metabolites accumulate and dilate arterioles, matching supply to demand. Flow to a working muscle can rise more than twentyfold.
  • Blushing — emotional stimuli withdraw sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone from facial skin.
  • Sites of acute inflammation — histamine and other mediators dilate arterioles, delivering leukocytes, antibodies and complement to the injured tissue.
  • Post-ischemic reactive hyperemia — after a temporary occlusion is released, accumulated metabolites cause a transient flow overshoot.

Hyperemia is beneficial. It is a physiological, adaptive response that raises delivery of oxygen, nutrients and immune effectors exactly where they are needed, and it resolves when the stimulus stops. This is a genuine conceptual difference from congestion, which is always pathological.

1.3 Congestion — the Passive, Venous Process

Definition

Congestion (venous or passive hyperemia) is a passive increase in local blood volume caused by impaired venous outflow from a tissue.

Nothing is actively dilating here. Blood simply cannot leave, so it backs up. This is the key to every downstream consequence: the stagnant blood in the bed has already given up its oxygen to the tissue, so the bed is filled with deoxygenated haemoglobin.

Etiology — three headings

Table 1.1   Causes of congestion, from the vessel outwards
CategoryMechanismRepresentative examples
Compression on the vein An external mass squeezes the thin, low-pressure venous wall shut. Veins collapse far more easily than arteries because their walls are thin and their intraluminal pressure is low. Tumour, gravid uterus on the IVC, tight cast or bandage, constricting scar, volvulus and hernia strangulating mesenteric veins
Obstruction within the vein The lumen itself is plugged, most often by thrombus. Deep vein thrombosis, portal vein thrombosis, hepatic vein thrombosis (Budd–Chiari syndrome), IVC tumour thrombus from renal cell carcinoma
Cardiac failure The pump cannot clear the blood delivered to it, so pressure rises retrogradely through the entire venous bed behind the failing chamber. This is the only systemic (generalised) cause. Left heart failure → pulmonary congestion; right heart failure → hepatic, splenic, renal and lower-limb congestion
Mechanism — why congested tissue is blue, cool and swollen
  1. Venous outflow is obstructed (compression, thrombus, or a failing heart behind it).The venous side is the only low-resistance escape route for capillary blood; block it and the blood has nowhere else to go.
  2. Blood accumulates and pressure rises retrogradely into the capillaries and venules.Pressure in a closed hydraulic system transmits backwards from the point of obstruction.
  3. The bed distends with blood that has already perfused the tissue and is therefore desaturated.Deoxyhaemoglobin is dark blue-purple; ≥5 g/dL of it in the capillary bed is visible through the skin as cyanosis.
  4. Because blood is stagnant, transit time is long and the tissue extracts a greater fraction of the oxygen still present, deepening the desaturation further.Slow flow gives each red cell more time at the tissue interface — extraction per unit volume rises even as total delivery falls.
  5. Delivery of warm core blood per minute falls, so the tissue cools.Convective heat delivery is proportional to flow, and flow is now reduced despite the increased standing volume.
  6. The raised capillary hydrostatic pressure drives water and salts across the capillary wall into the interstitium.This is the Starling equation acting directly — see §1.7. It is why congestion and edema almost always travel together.
  7. Chronic stagnation causes hypoxia of the parenchymal cells, capillary rupture with small haemorrhages, and ultimately scarring.Cells at the far (most distal) end of the perfusion path see the lowest oxygen tension and die first; their loss is repaired by fibrosis, not regeneration.
A tissue that is blue-red (cyanotic), cool, swollen and heavy, with reduced metabolism and function, and — if the process persists — haemorrhage, haemosiderin deposition, parenchymal atrophy and fibrosis.

Gross morphology of any congested organ

Four features, and each follows directly from the mechanism above:

  • Increased in size and weight — the organ is holding extra blood and extra interstitial fluid.
  • Firm (solidified) consistency — distended vessels and edematous interstitium resist deformation.
  • Dark red / blue-purple colour — the pooled blood is deoxygenated.
  • Tense, stretched capsule — the swollen parenchyma pushes outward against an inelastic capsule; this is why an acutely congested liver is painful (capsular stretch).

On sectioning, a congested organ oozes dark blood freely from the cut surface.

Clinical Correlation — varicose veins

Chronically raised intraluminal pressure in the superficial leg veins stretches them until their valves no longer meet. Once a valve is incompetent, standing blood is no longer broken into short columns by the valve cusps; the full hydrostatic column from the right atrium to the ankle now bears on the vein wall, which dilates further — a self-reinforcing loop. The result is chronic lower-limb congestion with stasis dermatitis, brown haemosiderin staining of the skin, and poorly-healing varicose ulcers. Note the exam point: embolism from superficial varicose veins is rare, whereas embolism from deep vein thrombosis is common and dangerous.

Exam Trap

Both processes are sometimes called "hyperemia" in older texts (hence "venous hyperemia" for congestion). If a question offers both "hyperemia" and "congestion" as options for a cyanotic, cool, swollen leg with a DVT, the answer is congestion. Read the qualifier: active = arterial = hyperemia; passive/venous = congestion.

1.5 The Congested Lung

The pulmonary circulation drains into the left atrium. Anything that raises left atrial pressure — failing left ventricle, mitral stenosis, mitral regurgitation — is transmitted backwards into the pulmonary veins and capillaries. Pulmonary congestion is therefore the signature lesion of left-sided heart disease.

Acute pulmonary congestion

Gross: heavy, wet, dark red-purple lungs. The cut surface oozes frothy, blood-tinged fluid — frothy because edema fluid is being whipped with air in the airways.

Microscopic — three findings: (i) alveolar septal capillaries are dilated and packed with red cells; (ii) protein-poor edema fluid (transudate) fills alveolar spaces, staining pale pink and homogeneous; (iii) scattered red cells lie free within alveoli where capillaries have leaked or ruptured.

Chronic pulmonary congestion and the heart failure cell

When left heart failure persists for months, the lung changes from a passively wet organ into a structurally remodelled one. The classical name is brown induration of the lung: brown from haemosiderin, induration (hardening) from fibrosis.

Mechanism — how a failing left ventricle produces heart failure cells
  1. The left ventricle fails, so it cannot eject its full end-diastolic volume. Residual volume accumulates and left ventricular end-diastolic pressure rises.A weakened pump ejects a smaller fraction of what it receives; what is not ejected stays behind and raises filling pressure.
  2. That pressure is transmitted backwards: left ventricle → left atrium → pulmonary veins → pulmonary capillaries.During diastole the mitral valve is open, so the ventricle, atrium and pulmonary veins form one continuous column of fluid; pressure equilibrates along it.
  3. Pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure rises above the plasma oncotic pressure, and fluid is forced into the alveolar septa and then into the airspaces.Net filtration = (hydrostatic gradient) − (oncotic gradient). Once hydrostatic pressure exceeds ~25 mmHg, the oncotic pull of albumin can no longer hold the fluid in.
  4. The same pressure over-distends thin-walled septal capillaries until small numbers of them rupture, releasing red blood cells into the alveolar space.An alveolar capillary wall is ~0.5 µm thick — built for gas exchange, not for pressure. Chronic over-distension causes microscopic breaks.
  5. Alveolar macrophages phagocytose the extravasated red cells.Free red cells in the airspace are debris; clearing them is the resident macrophage's job.
  6. Inside the phagolysosome, haemoglobin is degraded. The globin is recycled and the porphyrin ring is opened, but the iron cannot be excreted, so it is stored as ferritin aggregates that condense into haemosiderin, a coarse, golden-brown, refractile pigment.The body has no route for excreting iron in quantity; the only options are re-export or local storage. A macrophage sitting in an alveolus cannot export it efficiently, so it accumulates.
  7. These pigment-stuffed macrophages are the heart failure cells (siderophages). They accumulate in alveoli and can be coughed up in sputum.They are simply macrophages that have eaten too much blood; the name reflects the clinical context in which they are found, not a unique cell type.
  8. In parallel, chronic hypoxia and the mechanical stress of persistent edema stimulate septal fibroblasts to lay down collagen, and the septa thicken.Hypoxia and stretch are both profibrotic stimuli; the lung repairs recurrent low-grade injury with scar because alveolar architecture cannot be perfectly regenerated.
Brown induration of the lung — firm, brown, heavy lungs with rust-coloured spots on the cut surface; microscopically, thickened fibrotic alveolar septa plus haemosiderin-laden macrophages in the alveoli. Functionally, the thickened septum impairs gas diffusion, so the patient becomes dyspnoeic.
High-Yield — three features of chronic pulmonary congestion
  1. Thickened, fibrotic alveolar septa (the "induration").
  2. Haemosiderin-laden macrophages (heart failure cells) in the alveolar spaces (the "brown").
  3. Dilated, congested septal capillaries with variable interstitial edema.

Grossly: firm, heavy, brown lungs with rust-coloured spots. This is the lecture's named learning objective — expect it verbatim.

Clinical Correlation

A patient with chronic left heart failure or mitral stenosis presents with exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea and a cough productive of rust-coloured or blood-tinged sputum containing heart failure cells. In long-standing mitral stenosis, the chronically raised pulmonary venous pressure is eventually transmitted to the pulmonary arteries, producing pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular hypertrophy and finally right heart failure — left-sided disease that has "crossed over" to become right-sided. Note the sequence: chronic congestion of the lung also predisposes to red infarction after a pulmonary embolus, because the compensatory bronchial supply is already inadequate (see §5.3).

1.6 The Congested Liver and Nutmeg Liver

The hepatic veins drain into the inferior vena cava and thence into the right atrium. Hepatic congestion is therefore the signature lesion of right-sided heart failure (and of any obstruction of hepatic venous outflow, such as Budd–Chiari syndrome).

The essential anatomy — why the centre dies first

Blood enters the liver lobule at its periphery, from the portal triads (portal venule + hepatic arteriole), flows inward along the sinusoids, and exits at the centre through the central vein. Oxygen is extracted all the way along that path. Therefore:

Zone 1 (periportal) — highest pO2 — Zone 2 — Zone 3 (centrilobular) — lowest pO2, dies first

Two facts follow, and between them they explain the whole of nutmeg liver: (i) the centrilobular (zone 3) hepatocytes are the most hypoxia-vulnerable cells in the lobule because they see blood that is already partly deoxygenated; and (ii) the central vein is the first structure to feel back-pressure from the right heart, because it is the most downstream point in the lobule.

Mechanism — how the nutmeg pattern is built, step by step
  1. The right ventricle fails (or the hepatic veins are obstructed), so systemic venous pressure rises.The right heart is the end of the systemic venous circuit; when it cannot empty, everything behind it becomes engorged.
  2. Pressure is transmitted backwards: right atrium → inferior vena cava → hepatic veins → central veins of the lobules.These form an uninterrupted low-pressure column; there is no valve between the right atrium and the hepatic sinusoids to interrupt transmission.
  3. The central veins and the sinusoids immediately around them dilate and fill with dark venous blood.They are the thinnest-walled, most distensible and most downstream vessels in the lobule, so they yield to the raised pressure first.
  4. Sinusoidal flow slows to a crawl, so oxygen delivery to zone 3 falls below the level the hepatocytes need.Those cells were already at the lowest pO₂ in the lobule; any further reduction crosses their threshold for survival while zone 1 cells, bathed in fresh portal and arterial blood, are still comfortable.
  5. Centrilobular hepatocytes undergo hypoxic injury: first fatty change and atrophy, then, if the hypoxia is severe or prolonged, centrilobular haemorrhagic necrosis.Hypoxia halts oxidative phosphorylation; fatty acids can no longer be β-oxidised and accumulate as fat, and ATP depletion eventually causes irreversible membrane failure and cell death. The necrotic zone is haemorrhagic because it is simultaneously full of pooled blood.
  6. Meanwhile the periportal (zone 1) hepatocytes survive, because they receive blood first. They commonly develop fatty change from milder hypoxia and from the chronic illness itself, which turns them yellow-tan.Fatty change is the reversible, "lesser" hypoxic injury; it stops short of necrosis and gives the surviving tissue a pale yellow colour that contrasts sharply with the dark centre.
  7. The cut surface therefore shows dark red centrilobular foci set in a pale yellow-tan periportal background, repeated in every lobule across the whole organ.The lobule is a repeating unit, so a lesion that targets one zone of the lobule produces a regular, uniform mottling of the entire cut surface rather than a patchy lesion.
A cut surface that looks like a bisected nutmeg. The dark red = congested, necrotic centrilobular zones. The pale tan = surviving, often fatty, periportal parenchyma. If congestion persists for years, collagen is laid down around the central veins — cardiac sclerosis, sometimes progressing to true cardiac cirrhosis.
Exam Trap — which part of the nutmeg is dark?

Students routinely invert this. The dark red areas are centrilobular (around the central vein) — congested and necrotic. The pale areas are periportal — surviving. The logic is unambiguous once you remember that blood backs up from the central vein, so congestion must start there. A second trap: nutmeg liver is a lesion of right heart failure; brown induration of the lung is a lesion of left heart failure. Do not mix the sides.

High-Yield — three features of nutmeg liver
  1. Dilated, congested central veins and perivenular sinusoids.
  2. Centrilobular hepatocyte atrophy, degeneration and haemorrhagic necrosis.
  3. Periportal hepatocytes preserved, commonly with fatty change — producing the pale component of the mottling.

Clinically: tender hepatomegaly, raised jugular venous pressure, peripheral edema, ascites, and mildly raised transaminases. In acute severe right heart failure the transaminase rise can be dramatic ("ischemic hepatitis" / shock liver).

1.7 Edema — Starling Forces and the Physiology of Leakage

Definition

Edema is an increase in fluid within the interstitial tissue spaces, or within body cavities. It is an extravascular accumulation — the water has left the vessel; the blood cells have not.

Roughly 60% of body weight is water, and about two-thirds of that is intracellular. Edema concerns the remaining extracellular compartment, and specifically the balance between its two sub-compartments — plasma (inside the vessel) and interstitium (outside it). The wall of the capillary is freely permeable to water and small solutes but relatively impermeable to plasma proteins. Movement of fluid across that wall is governed by four Starling forces:

Table 1.3   The four Starling forces
ForceDirectionComment
Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc)OUTThe blood pressure inside the capillary. ~32 mmHg at the arterial end, ~12 mmHg at the venous end. The force most often deranged in disease.
Plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure (πc)INGenerated almost entirely by albumin, which is too large to cross. ~25 mmHg, essentially constant along the capillary.
Interstitial hydrostatic pressure (Pi)INNormally near zero or slightly negative; rises as the interstitium fills, which is a natural brake on further edema.
Interstitial oncotic pressure (πi)OUTNormally low, because lymphatics continuously remove the few proteins that escape. Rises sharply in inflammation and in lymphatic obstruction.
Net filtration = Kf × [ (Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi) ]   →   edema whenever this exceeds what the lymphatics can carry away

In health there is a small net filtration of fluid into the interstitium — roughly 2–4 litres per day across the whole body — and the lymphatic system returns exactly that amount to the circulation via the thoracic duct. Edema therefore requires one of two things: an increase in filtration that exceeds lymphatic capacity, or a failure of lymphatic drainage. The lymphatics can up-regulate their flow perhaps tenfold, which is why modest increases in capillary pressure produce no visible edema at all.

High-Yield

Every cause of edema you will ever be asked about works by altering exactly one of four variables: ↑Pc, ↓πc, ↑lymphatic resistance, or ↑capillary permeability (which raises πi by letting protein out). Sodium retention is best understood as a mechanism that raises Pc by expanding plasma volume. If you can classify a scenario into one of these four, you have answered the question.

1.8 The Four Mechanisms of Non-inflammatory Edema

① Increased hydrostatic pressure

Anything that impedes venous return raises Pc upstream of the obstruction.

  • Local: deep vein thrombosis → unilateral leg edema; constrictive pericarditis; a tumour compressing a vein; prolonged standing or immobility.
  • Generalised: congestive heart failure — the single commonest cause of edema in clinical practice.
Mechanism — the vicious circle of edema in congestive heart failure
  1. Cardiac output falls because the ventricle cannot eject effectively.This is the primary defect; everything downstream is compensation or consequence.
  2. Blood dams up behind the ventricle, raising venous and hence capillary hydrostatic pressure. Fluid begins to filter into the interstitium.The Starling balance is tipped outward by the raised Pc.
  3. Renal perfusion falls, because cardiac output is low and because fluid has moved out of the vascular compartment.The kidney senses arterial underfilling, not total body water; it cannot distinguish "not enough blood" from "blood in the wrong compartment".
  4. The juxtaglomerular apparatus releases renin → angiotensin I → angiotensin II (via ACE) → aldosterone; ADH is also released.This is the body's standard response to perceived hypovolaemia — retain salt and water to restore circulating volume.
  5. The kidney retains sodium and water, expanding plasma volume.Aldosterone drives Na⁺ reabsorption in the distal nephron; water follows osmotically, assisted by ADH-driven aquaporin insertion.
  6. But the heart still cannot pump the extra volume, so the added fluid simply raises venous pressure further.Expanding the volume in front of a broken pump does not improve forward flow; it only increases the pressure behind it. This is the vicious circle.
  7. Edema worsens, arterial underfilling persists, renin release continues.The compensation is now the disease. This is exactly why diuretics and ACE inhibitors — which interrupt steps 4–5 — are cornerstone treatments in heart failure.
Progressive, dependent, pitting edema with raised jugular venous pressure, hepatomegaly and — eventually — ascites and anasarca.

② Reduced plasma oncotic pressure (hypoalbuminaemia)

Albumin is the molecule that holds water inside the vessel. When serum albumin falls below roughly 2.5 g/dL, the inward oncotic pull is no longer sufficient to balance even a normal hydrostatic pressure, and fluid leaves the circulation everywhere at once. The result is generalised edema, most obvious where tissues are loose — periorbital, scrotal, and eventually anasarca.

  • Excessive loss — nephrotic syndrome: damaged glomerular capillary walls leak albumin into the urine (>3.5 g/day). The classic presentation is periorbital edema worst on waking.
  • Reduced synthesis — cirrhosis (the diseased liver cannot make albumin) and protein malnutrition (kwashiorkor: no substrate to make it from). The protuberant abdomen of a child with kwashiorkor is ascites on a background of muscle wasting.
  • Protein-losing enteropathy — albumin is lost across a diseased gut mucosa.

The circle closes here too. Loss of albumin reduces intravascular volume → renal hypoperfusion → renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation → salt and water retention. But the retained fluid cannot be held in the vessel either, because albumin is still low. So it too leaks out, worsening the edema without correcting the underlying deficit.

③ Lymphatic obstruction — lymphedema

Here filtration is normal but drainage has failed. Because the lymphatics are also the route by which escaped plasma protein is returned to the blood, obstruction causes protein to accumulate in the interstitium. That raises interstitial oncotic pressure (πi), which pulls yet more water out — so lymphedema is self-perpetuating and protein-rich, which is why it becomes firm and non-pitting over time rather than staying soft.

  • Surgical — axillary lymph node dissection in breast cancer surgery; classic cause of arm lymphedema.
  • Neoplastic — tumour infiltrating lymphatic channels or nodes. In breast carcinoma, dermal lymphatic invasion tethers the skin and produces the dimpled peau d'orange ("orange peel") appearance — the skin is edematous but remains anchored at the hair follicles, which therefore appear as pits.
  • Post-radiation fibrosis; post-inflammatory scarring and thrombosis of lymphatics.
  • Filariasis — Wuchereria bancrofti. Adult nematodes live in and obstruct lymphatic channels, provoking fibrosing inflammation of inguinal nodes and lymphatics. Massive chronic lymphedema of the leg and scrotum with thickened, verrucous skin is elephantiasis.
  • Milroy disease — primary congenital lymphedema from agenesis or hypoplasia of lymphatics.

If obstructed lymphatics rupture, milky lymph accumulates in cavities: chylous ascites, chylothorax, chylopericardium.

④ Sodium and water retention

Salt is osmotically obliged to carry water with it. Retaining sodium therefore expands the extracellular fluid volume, which raises capillary hydrostatic pressure and simultaneously dilutes plasma proteins, lowering oncotic pressure. Both Starling forces move in the direction of edema at once — which is why this mechanism is so potent.

  • Primary renal failure — the damaged kidney simply cannot excrete the filtered sodium load (acute glomerulonephritis is the classic example; children present with periorbital edema, hypertension and cola-coloured urine).
  • Secondary — the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation described above in heart failure, cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome.
  • Excessive salt intake with renal insufficiency; some drugs (NSAIDs, corticosteroids, some antihypertensives).
Mnemonic — the four causes

"Pressure Out, Protein Low, Lymph Blocked, Salt Held"
Pressure (↑hydrostatic) · Protein (↓oncotic) · Lymph (obstruction) · Salt (Na⁺ retention). The fifth, inflammatory edema, belongs to a different category — see below.

1.9 Transudate versus Exudate

The four mechanisms above all produce a transudate: the capillary wall is intact, so only water and small solutes escape. Inflammatory edema is mechanistically different — chemical mediators (histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes, C3a/C5a) make the endothelium of post-capillary venules contract and pull apart, opening interendothelial gaps. Now protein and cells escape too, and the fluid is an exudate.

Same organ, same clinical picture of hypoxaemia and bilateral infiltrates, opposite mechanism and opposite treatment. If the vignette mentions sepsis, aspiration, trauma or pancreatitis with a normal heart, think ARDS.

Subcutaneous edema and the pitting sign

Dependent edema is the distribution characteristic of raised hydrostatic pressure: gravity adds to capillary pressure in whichever part of the body is lowest. In an ambulant patient this is the legs and ankles; in a bedbound patient it is the sacrum. Always examine the sacrum in a patient who has been lying down — leg edema may be absent there.

Periorbital edema, by contrast, points to hypoalbuminaemia or acute glomerulonephritis: the periorbital tissue is so loose that it distends with even small amounts of fluid, and it is not gravity-dependent. This is why nephrotic syndrome classically presents with puffy eyelids on waking.

Pitting versus non-pitting. Low-protein transudate is mobile and pits readily. Protein-rich lymphedema is viscous, and chronic lymphedema deposits collagen in the interstitium, so it becomes firm, woody ("brawny induration") and non-pitting. Myxoedema in hypothyroidism is also non-pitting, but for a different reason: the interstitium is filled with hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans, not free water.

Pulmonary edema

The lungs may weigh two to three times normal. Sectioning releases frothy, sometimes blood-tinged fluid — a mixture of air, edema fluid and extravasated red cells. Histologically, alveolar capillaries are engorged and the alveolar spaces contain a granular pink precipitate.

Causes: left ventricular failure (by far the commonest), mitral stenosis, renal failure with volume overload, ARDS, high-altitude exposure, and inflammatory or infectious lung disease. Consequences: fluid in the alveolus increases the diffusion distance for oxygen and dilutes surfactant, so the patient becomes hypoxaemic and the alveoli tend to collapse; it also creates an excellent culture medium, predisposing to secondary infection.

Cerebral edema

Cerebral edema is uniquely dangerous because the brain is enclosed in a rigid box. The Monro–Kellie doctrine states that the sum of brain, blood and cerebrospinal fluid volumes within the cranium is constant; if one increases, another must be displaced or intracranial pressure must rise. Initially CSF is displaced into the spinal subarachnoid space and venous blood is squeezed out — but these reserves are small, and once exhausted, pressure rises steeply.

Clinical features: headache (worse in the morning and on coughing or straining), nausea and vomiting, papilloedema, falling level of consciousness, and finally herniation — subfalcine, transtentorial (uncal) or tonsillar through the foramen magnum. Tonsillar herniation compresses the medulla and is rapidly fatal because it destroys the respiratory and cardiovascular centres.

High-Yield — reading an edema vignette in three questions
  1. Is it local or generalised? Local → obstruction (venous or lymphatic) on that side. Generalised → heart, liver, kidney.
  2. Does it pit? Pitting → low-protein transudate (hydrostatic or oncotic). Non-pitting → protein-rich lymphedema, or myxoedema.
  3. Where is it worst? Legs/sacrum (dependent) → hydrostatic, i.e. heart failure. Periorbital → hypoalbuminaemia or acute glomerulonephritis. One arm after mastectomy → lymphatic.

Hemorrhage

Edema is water leaving the vessel through an intact wall. Hemorrhage is whole blood leaving it through a breach. The clinical question is never simply "is there bleeding?" but "how much, how fast, and where?" — because those three variables, not the diagnosis, determine whether the patient is inconvenienced or dead.

By the end of this Part you should be able to

  • Define hemorrhage and distinguish rupture (per rhexis) from diapedesis.
  • Apply the size-based terminology — petechia, purpura, ecchymosis — correctly and state the measurements.
  • Explain the colour sequence of a resolving bruise from first principles of haem catabolism.
  • Predict the consequence of a haemorrhage from its volume, rate and anatomical site.

2.1 Definition and the Two Mechanisms of Escape

Definition

Hemorrhage is the extravasation of blood from the cardiovascular system into the extravascular space, a body cavity, or to the exterior.

Note what is and is not implied. Hemorrhage is not defined by volume — a petechia is a hemorrhage. It is not defined by visibility — a retroperitoneal bleed is invisible until the patient collapses. It is defined by whole blood, including cells, having left the vascular compartment. That is the difference from edema, where only the fluid phase escapes.

2.2 Causes of Hemorrhage

  • Trauma — the commonest cause overall: laceration, crush, penetrating injury, fracture.
  • Vascular wall weakness — aneurysm (berry aneurysm of the circle of Willis, abdominal aortic aneurysm, Charcot–Bouchard microaneurysms in chronic hypertension), atherosclerosis, vasculitis, and connective tissue disorders such as Marfan or Ehlers–Danlos syndrome.
  • Erosion by disease — a peptic ulcer eroding the gastroduodenal artery; a bronchogenic carcinoma invading a pulmonary vessel (haemoptysis); tuberculous cavitation.
  • Bleeding disorders (haemorrhagic diatheses) — three headings:
    • Platelet — thrombocytopenia (ITP, TTP, DIC, marrow failure, hypersplenism) or platelet dysfunction (uraemia, aspirin, Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard–Soulier).
    • Coagulation factor — haemophilia A (factor VIII) and B (factor IX), von Willebrand disease, vitamin K deficiency, liver failure, warfarin.
    • Vessel wall — scurvy (defective collagen), Henoch–Schönlein purpura, amyloidosis, senile purpura, chronic corticosteroid use.
  • Severe congestion / hypoxia — diapedetic haemorrhage as described above.
High-Yield — platelet problem or factor problem?

The pattern of bleeding tells you which arm of hemostasis has failed, before any laboratory test.
Primary hemostasis (platelet/vWF) defect → mucocutaneous bleeding: petechiae, purpura, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, menorrhagia, easy bruising. Bleeding starts immediately after injury because the platelet plug never forms.
Secondary hemostasis (coagulation factor) defect → deep bleeding: haemarthrosis, deep muscle haematomas, rebleeding hours after an initially controlled injury, because the weak platelet plug forms but is never reinforced with fibrin and then breaks down.

2.4 Why a Bruise Changes Colour

The colour sequence of a resolving ecchymosis is not a curiosity — it is haem catabolism happening in front of you, and it lets you date an injury, which matters in forensic and paediatric practice.

Mechanism — the colour clock of a bruise
  1. Day 0–1: red-purple. Extravasated red cells are intact and their haemoglobin is still largely oxygenated, then desaturates.You are simply looking at blood through skin. As the trapped blood loses its oxygen, the colour deepens from red to blue-purple.
  2. Day 1–5: blue-black. Red cells lyse; free haemoglobin is phagocytosed by macrophages recruited to the site.Extravasated red cells have no supporting environment and undergo haemolysis within a day or two; macrophages arrive as part of the normal clean-up response.
  3. Day 5–7: green. Inside the macrophage, haem oxygenase opens the porphyrin ring of haem, releasing iron and carbon monoxide and producing biliverdin, which is green.Haem oxygenase is the rate-limiting enzyme of haem degradation; biliverdin is its immediate, green-coloured product.
  4. Day 7–10: yellow-brown. Biliverdin reductase converts biliverdin to bilirubin, which is yellow.This is the same reaction that occurs in the spleen during normal red cell turnover — the bruise is simply doing it locally and visibly.
  5. Day 10–14+: golden-brown, fading. The iron liberated in step 3 is stored as haemosiderin, a golden-brown pigment, while bilirubin diffuses away and is cleared.Bilirubin is soluble and leaves; iron is not excretable and stays until macrophages slowly carry it off — which is why the brown stain is the last colour to disappear, and why chronic venous congestion of the leg leaves permanent brown skin staining.
Red-purple → blue-black → green → yellow → golden-brown. Haemoglobin → biliverdin → bilirubin → haemosiderin. The same pathway, in the same order, every time.
Mnemonic

"Really Bad Grapes Yield Brown" — Red · Blue · Green · Yellow · Brown.

Clinical Correlation — massive haemorrhage and jaundice

A very large haematoma — a retroperitoneal bleed, or a huge subgaleal haemorrhage in a newborn — can produce clinically obvious jaundice a few days later. The reason follows directly from the mechanism above: breaking down a large mass of extravasated red cells generates a bilirubin load that outstrips the liver's conjugating capacity, giving an unconjugated (indirect) hyperbilirubinaemia. This is a favourite exam scenario in neonates with cephalohaematoma.

② Rate

The same volume lost slowly and lost quickly produce entirely different pictures.

  • Rapid loss → hypovolaemic shock. There is no time for compensation; the problem is loss of circulating volume and therefore of cardiac output. Note that haemoglobin concentration may initially be normal, because red cells and plasma are lost together — the haematocrit falls only after hours, as interstitial fluid shifts into the vascular space to refill it.
  • Slow, chronic loss (occult colonic carcinoma, menorrhagia, peptic ulcer oozing) → iron-deficiency anaemia. The presentation is fatigue, pallor and a microcytic, hypochromic blood film — not shock. In an adult male or postmenopausal female, iron-deficiency anaemia is gastrointestinal malignancy until proven otherwise.

③ Site

This is the variable that overrides the other two. A volume of blood that would be trivial in the thigh is lethal in the wrong place, because the damage is mechanical, not volumetric.

  • Brainstem — a few millilitres destroy the respiratory and cardiovascular centres. Fatal.
  • Pericardial sac — 200–300 mL acutely causes tamponade and obstructive shock.
  • Intracranial (subdural, extradural, intracerebral) — raised intracranial pressure and herniation.
  • Airway (retropharyngeal, laryngeal) — asphyxia from a small volume.
  • Skin and subcutaneous tissue — the same volume is merely a bruise.
Exam Trap

Do not reach for haemoglobin to assess an acute bleed. In the first hours it is falsely reassuring, because whole blood is lost — cells and plasma in the same proportion — so the concentration is unchanged. The early indicators are heart rate and pulse pressure, not haematocrit. Conversely, a low haemoglobin with a normal blood pressure and no tachycardia points to chronic loss.

Thrombosis

Hemostasis and thrombosis are the same process. The difference is entirely contextual: hemostasis is clot formation at a site of vascular injury, where it is needed; thrombosis is clot formation inside an intact vessel, where it is not. You cannot understand the pathology without first understanding the physiology, so this Part begins with normal hemostasis and only then asks what goes wrong.

  • Walk through the four stages of hemostasis and say what each contributes.
  • Explain each arm of Virchow's triad with a clinical example, and say how each arm actually promotes clotting.
  • Compare white, mixed, red and hyaline thrombi on composition, location and gross appearance.
  • Recognise lines of Zahn and explain why they prove the thrombus formed in flowing blood before death.
  • List the four fates of a thrombus and describe the mechanism of organization and recanalization.

3.1 Normal Hemostasis — the Four-Step Sequence

Definition

Hemostasis is a precisely orchestrated process involving platelets, clotting factors and endothelium that occurs at a site of vascular injury and culminates in a blood clot that prevents or limits bleeding.

Mechanism — the four stages, and why each is necessary
  1. Arteriolar vasoconstriction. Reflex neurogenic constriction is amplified by endothelin secreted by the injured endothelium.Reducing flow buys time: it limits the rate of blood loss and, crucially, keeps activated platelets and clotting factors in the local area long enough to reach effective concentrations. Without this, everything downstream would be washed away. The effect is transient, so it alone is never sufficient.
  2. Primary hemostasis — the platelet plug. Injury exposes subendothelial collagen and von Willebrand factor. Platelets adhere, change shape from smooth discs into flat plates with spiky protrusions, release their granules and aggregate.The shape change is not cosmetic: it massively increases the surface area available both for platelet-to-platelet contact and for assembly of coagulation factor complexes. The plug forms within seconds — fast enough to stop bleeding from small vessels on its own.
  3. Secondary hemostasis — fibrin deposition. Tissue factor exposed at the injury site activates the coagulation cascade, generating thrombin. Thrombin cleaves circulating fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin, creating a meshwork, and is itself a potent platelet activator.The platelet plug alone is friable and would be swept away by arterial pressure. Fibrin is the reinforcing mesh — the steel in the concrete. Note thrombin's dual role: it makes fibrin and recruits more platelets, so the two arms of hemostasis amplify each other.
  4. Clot stabilisation and resorption. Polymerised fibrin is covalently cross-linked by factor XIIIa; platelet contraction consolidates the mass. Counter-regulatory mechanisms (t-PA, thrombomodulin) then confine the clot to the site of injury and eventually dissolve it.Cross-linking converts a reversible gel into a permanent solid plug. Counter-regulation is equally essential: without it, a clot triggered by a paper cut would propagate down the whole limb.
A solid, permanent plug that prevents further bleeding, localised precisely to the injured segment, and programmed for eventual removal once the wall has healed.
High-Yield

Thrombosis is hemostasis in the wrong place. The molecular machinery is identical. What differs is that in thrombosis, the trigger is an abnormality of the endothelium, the flow, or the blood itself — Virchow's triad — rather than a genuine wound.

3.2 Endothelium — the Switch

An intact endothelial monolayer is actively anti-thrombotic — not merely a passive barrier. This is a critical conceptual point: a resting endothelial cell is continuously working to keep blood fluid. When it is injured or activated, that work stops and the opposite programme begins. The integrity and functional state of the endothelium determine whether clots form, propagate or dissolve.

This is one of the most commonly missed points in the whole topic. A thrombus can form over an endothelium that is morphologically intact but functionally activated — no cells have been stripped away, yet the surface has switched from anti- to pro-thrombotic. Hypertension, turbulent flow, bacterial endotoxin, hypercholesterolaemia, cigarette smoke, homocysteine and inflammatory cytokines all do this. It explains why atherosclerotic plaques thrombose without visible ulceration, and why a "normal-looking" vessel on histology can still be the origin of a thrombus.

3.3 Primary Hemostasis — Adhesion, Activation, Aggregation

Mechanism — building the primary hemostatic plug
  1. Adhesion. Subendothelial collagen is exposed. Plasma von Willebrand factor binds the collagen, changes conformation, and is then bound by platelet receptor GpIb.vWF is a molecular bridge, and it is indispensable under high shear. Platelets cannot bind collagen directly at arterial flow rates — they would be torn off. vWF's elongation under shear creates a catch-bond that grips harder the faster the blood moves. This is why von Willebrand disease and Bernard–Soulier syndrome (absent GpIb) both cause mucocutaneous bleeding.
  2. Activation and shape change. The adherent platelet converts from a smooth disc into a flattened cell with spiky protrusions, and flips negatively charged phosphatidylserine to its outer membrane leaflet.The spikes increase surface area for contact. The exposed phosphatidylserine is the platform on which the calcium-dependent coagulation complexes (tenase and prothrombinase) assemble — which is exactly how primary hemostasis hands over to secondary hemostasis.
  3. Secretion (the release reaction). α-granules release fibrinogen, factor V, vWF, fibronectin and PDGF; dense granules release ADP, calcium, ATP and serotonin (5-HT). The platelet also synthesises thromboxane A2 (TXA2) from membrane arachidonic acid via cyclo-oxygenase.ADP and TXA₂ are the two key autocrine/paracrine amplifiers: they activate neighbouring platelets, turning a single adhesion event into a growing mass. This is also the pharmacological pressure point — aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX-1 and blocks TXA₂; clopidogrel blocks the P2Y12 ADP receptor.
  4. Aggregation. ADP and TXA2 induce a conformational change in platelet integrin GpIIb/IIIa, which now binds fibrinogen. Each fibrinogen molecule bridges two platelets.Fibrinogen is bivalent, so it physically links platelets into a mass. Glanzmann thrombasthenia (absent GpIIb/IIIa) blocks this step; so do the drugs abciximab, eptifibatide and tirofiban.
  5. Consolidation. The first wave of aggregation is reversible. Thrombin, generated concurrently by the coagulation cascade, then (a) activates more platelets, (b) drives irreversible platelet contraction via the cytoskeleton, and (c) converts fibrinogen to fibrin and activates factor XIIIa, which covalently cross-links the fibrin.Reversibility early on is a safety feature — it allows an inappropriate aggregate to disperse. Thrombin is the commitment signal: once it arrives, the plug becomes the definitive secondary hemostatic plug and cannot disassemble.
A contracted, fibrin-cemented platelet mass firmly anchored to the vessel wall.

3.4 Secondary Hemostasis — the Coagulation Cascade

The cascade is a series of amplifying proenzyme-to-enzyme conversions. Each activated factor cleaves many molecules of the next, so a tiny initiating stimulus produces an explosive burst of thrombin. Each reaction requires a phospholipid surface (supplied by the activated platelet) and calcium — which is precisely how the cascade is confined to the site where platelets have already gathered.

EXTRINSIC: Tissue factor + VII → VIIa → X
INTRINSIC: XII → XIIa → XI → XIa → IX → IXa (+ VIIIa, PL, Ca2+) → X
COMMON: Xa (+ Va, PL, Ca2+) → Prothrombin (II) → Thrombin (IIa) → Fibrinogen (I) → Fibrin → (XIIIa) → Cross-linked fibrin

Why two pathways, and what the laboratory tests actually measure

The division into "intrinsic" and "extrinsic" is an in vitro construct — it reflects how the two clotting-time assays are performed, not two independent biological routes. In vivo, the tissue factor (extrinsic) pathway is the dominant initiator; the intrinsic pathway's main physiological role is amplification, with thrombin itself feeding back to activate factors XI, VIII and V. Nevertheless the division is clinically indispensable, because it is what makes PT and PTT interpretable.

Thrombin — the central enzyme

Of all the coagulation factors, thrombin is the most important. It is not merely the enzyme that makes fibrin; it is the hub through which coagulation, platelet activation, inflammation and even repair are coordinated. Six actions worth knowing:

  1. Converts fibrinogen → fibrin, and activates factor XIII to cross-link it.
  2. Activates platelets through protease-activated receptors (PAR-1) — the most potent physiological platelet agonist.
  3. Amplifies its own generation by activating factors V, VIII and XI.
  4. Pro-inflammatory — activates leukocytes and endothelium via PARs, linking thrombosis to inflammation.
  5. Anticoagulant, when bound to thrombomodulin on intact endothelium: the complex activates protein C, which (with protein S) inactivates factors Va and VIIIa. The same molecule thus switches from clot-promoting to clot-limiting depending on where it is.
  6. Promotes repair — stimulates fibroblast proliferation and smooth muscle migration, which is the first step of thrombus organization (§3.12).

3.6 Virchow's Triad — the Three Roads to Thrombosis

Definition

Thrombosis is the formation of an adherent clotted mass of blood within the cardiovascular system of a living body. The mass itself is a thrombus.

Every word in that definition is load-bearing. Adherent — it is attached to the wall, which distinguishes it from a post-mortem clot. Within the cardiovascular system — it can arise in cardiac chambers, on valve cusps, in arteries, veins or capillaries. In a living body — a clot formed after death is not a thrombus.

① Endothelial injury

The dominant influence, and the one that can cause thrombosis by itself, without help from the other two. It is particularly important in the heart and arteries, where rapid flow would normally prevent platelets adhering and would dilute activated clotting factors — so something must actively overcome that protection.

Mechanism — how endothelial injury initiates a thrombus
  1. The anti-thrombotic surface is lost or switched off (physical denudation, or functional activation by cytokines, endotoxin, hyperlipidaemia, smoking, hypertension, homocysteine or radiation).Recall from §3.2 that the endothelium is actively anti-thrombotic. Losing that activity alone is enough to shift the balance.
  2. Subendothelial collagen and von Willebrand factor become accessible.These are the ligands for platelet GpIa/IIa and GpIb — the physical trigger for adhesion.
  3. Platelets adhere, activate, release ADP, TXA2 and serotonin, and aggregate.This is the release reaction of §3.3, now occurring where there is no wound to seal.
  4. Tissue factor is expressed by the injured endothelium and by any exposed subendothelial cells, triggering the extrinsic cascade; the activated platelet surface provides the phospholipid for the cascade complexes.Tissue factor is the physiological initiator; the platelet supplies the assembly platform. Neither works efficiently without the other, which is why the two arms of hemostasis are obligatorily coupled.
  5. Thrombin is generated; fibrinogen is converted to fibrin, which traps more platelets and red cells; the mass enlarges and becomes adherent.Once fibrin cross-links, the thrombus is anchored to the wall and can no longer be swept away — it is now committed to grow or to be organized.
Clinical causes of endothelial injury
  • Ulcerated atherosclerotic plaque — the single most important cause of arterial thrombosis; plaque rupture exposes the highly thrombogenic lipid core.
  • Myocardial infarction — injured endocardium overlying the infarct is a nidus for mural thrombus.
  • Valvular injury — rheumatic disease, infective endocarditis, prosthetic valves.
  • Vasculitis; trauma and surgery; indwelling catheters.
  • Hypertension and turbulent flow — chronic mechanical stress activates endothelium without stripping it.
  • Metabolic and toxic — hypercholesterolaemia, homocystinuria, cigarette smoke, radiation, bacterial endotoxin.

② Abnormal blood flow — stasis and turbulence

Normal blood flow is laminar: it moves in concentric layers, fastest in the centre, and the cellular elements are confined to that central stream. A cell-free plasmatic zone separates them from the endothelium. Anything that destroys laminar flow removes that separation.

Mechanism — six ways abnormal flow promotes thrombosis
  1. It disrupts laminar flow and brings platelets into contact with the endothelium.The protective plasmatic zone is abolished, so platelets can now physically reach the wall.
  2. It prevents dilution of activated clotting factors.In normal flow, activated factors are swept away and diluted below the threshold needed to propagate the cascade. In stasis they accumulate locally until that threshold is crossed.
  3. It retards the inflow of clotting factor inhibitors.Antithrombin III, protein C and TFPI must be delivered by flowing blood. If fresh blood does not arrive, the brakes are never applied.
  4. It permits the build-up of platelet aggregates and nascent fibrin in the sluggish stream or in pockets of stasis.Small aggregates that would normally be dispersed by shear are allowed to consolidate.
  5. It promotes endothelial hypoxia and injury, causing platelet and fibrin deposition and reducing release of t-PA.Endothelial cells depend on flowing blood for oxygen; hypoxic endothelium switches to the pro-thrombotic phenotype and stops secreting fibrinolytic t-PA.
  6. Turbulence itself is a mechanism of endothelial injury.Disturbed shear stress alters endothelial gene expression, up-regulating adhesion molecules and tissue factor. This is why the triad's arms are interlinked rather than separate.

③ Hypercoagulability

Any alteration of the blood — most often of the clotting mechanism — that predisposes to thrombosis. Of all the coagulation factors, thrombin is the most important. Hypercoagulability is the least common of the three arms as a sole cause, but it is the one you must actively look for in a young patient or an unusual site of thrombosis.

3.8 Morphology — Lines of Zahn and the Four Thrombus Types

Lines of Zahn

Named after the Swiss–German pathologist Friedrich Wilhelm Zahn, these are alternating pale and dark laminations within a thrombus: pale layers of platelets and fibrin alternating with dark, red-cell-rich layers.

Mechanism — why the laminations form, and why they matter
  1. A thrombus begins at a point of endothelial injury and grows into the lumen against flowing blood.Growth is not uniform — it happens in waves, driven by the pulsatile nature of flow.
  2. Platelets, being small and light, are preferentially deposited on the leading surface during rapid flow, forming a pale layer.At high shear, platelets marginate toward the vessel wall and the growing thrombus surface, while the bulkier red cells remain in the central stream.
  3. Fibrin then forms across that platelet layer, trapping the red cells that happen to be passing when flow momentarily slows — producing a dark layer.Red cells are trapped passively, in proportion to how slow the stream is at that instant.
  4. The cycle repeats with each variation in flow, building up visible laminations.The alternation is a direct recording of the pulse — which is exactly why it is diagnostic.
Lines of Zahn prove the thrombus formed in flowing blood in a living body — i.e. it is a true ante-mortem thrombus, not a post-mortem clot. They are most conspicuous in the heart and aorta, where flow is fastest; in slow venous flow they are present but poorly developed.

The four morphological types

A venous thrombus classically grows as a propagating structure with three parts: the head attached to the wall (white thrombus), the body (mixed thrombus) and the free-floating tail (red thrombus). The tail is the dangerous part — it is poorly anchored and most likely to break off as an embolus.

3.11 Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation

DIC is not a disease but a complication of another condition. It is the paradox of pathology: widespread thrombosis that causes bleeding.

Mechanism — why DIC causes thrombosis and bleeding
  1. A trigger releases massive amounts of tissue factor or causes widespread endothelial injury.Classic triggers: obstetric complications (abruptio placentae, amniotic fluid embolism, retained dead fetus), Gram-negative sepsis, massive trauma or burns, disseminated malignancy (especially acute promyelocytic leukaemia and mucinous adenocarcinoma), and severe transfusion reactions.
  2. The coagulation cascade is activated throughout the circulation at once, not at a single site.Normally the cascade is localised by the phospholipid surface requirement and by the natural anticoagulants. A systemic trigger overwhelms both.
  3. Fibrin microthrombi (hyaline thrombi) form in capillaries and small vessels everywhere — especially kidney, lung, brain, liver, adrenal and skin.Capillaries are where flow is slowest and surface-to-volume ratio highest, so they occlude first.
  4. This causes widespread microvascular ischaemia and microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia — red cells are sheared into schistocytes as they are forced through fibrin strands.A fibrin mesh across a capillary acts like a cheese wire on a passing red cell.
  5. Meanwhile, platelets and clotting factors are consumed in making all that fibrin, and secondary fibrinolysis is activated, flooding the plasma with fibrin degradation products that further inhibit platelet function and fibrin polymerisation.The body's supply of coagulation substrate is finite. Once exhausted, the patient cannot clot at all — hence the alternative name, consumption coagulopathy.
  6. The patient now bleeds — from venepuncture sites, mucosae, surgical wounds and into the skin as petechiae and purpura — while simultaneously infarcting organs.Both phenomena have the same cause. This is why DIC is so difficult to treat: anticoagulating worsens the bleeding, transfusing clotting factors feeds the thrombosis. Treatment must be directed at the underlying trigger.
Laboratory picture: ↓platelets, ↑PT, ↑PTT, ↓fibrinogen, ↑↑D-dimer and fibrin degradation products, schistocytes on the blood film.

3.12 The Four Fates of a Thrombus

① Propagation

The thrombus enlarges by accreting more platelets and fibrin, often until it occludes a critical vessel. This is why anticoagulation is started immediately in DVT — not to dissolve the existing clot, but to stop it growing while the body's own fibrinolytic system works on it.

② Softening, liquefaction and resolution (lysis)

Plasmin, activated by t-PA, digests fibrin; leukocytic enzymes soften the mass; small thrombi may disappear completely. Efficacy depends critically on age: a fresh thrombus is rich in un-cross-linked fibrin and dissolves readily, whereas an old thrombus is extensively cross-linked by factor XIIIa and partly organized, and resists lysis. This is the entire rationale for the "time is muscle / time is brain" principle — thrombolytic therapy works only within a narrow window after clot formation.

③ Embolisation

The thrombus, or more usually its poorly-anchored tail, detaches and travels downstream. This is the bridge from Part 3 into Part 4, and the most feared outcome of a lower-limb DVT.

④ Organization and recanalization

Mechanism — turning a clot back into a channel
  1. Within 1–2 days, neutrophils and then macrophages infiltrate the thrombus and begin to digest it.A thrombus adherent to the wall is treated by the body as any other injury requiring repair — the inflammatory phase comes first.
  2. From day 2–3, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts grow in from the adjacent vessel wall.Growth factors released by platelets in the thrombus itself — PDGF, TGF-β — and by thrombin recruit exactly these cell types. The thrombus provides its own scaffold and its own stimulus.
  3. Over 1–2 weeks the ingrowing cells lay down collagen, converting the thrombus into a vascularised mass of granulation tissue — this is organization.This is ordinary wound healing, simply occurring inside a vessel lumen instead of across a skin wound.
  4. Capillary sprouts within the organizing mass elongate, anastomose and fuse into larger channels that eventually connect the two ends of the original lumen — recanalization.Angiogenesis follows the pressure gradient across the occlusion; channels that connect a high-pressure to a low-pressure end carry flow and are retained, while blind sprouts regress.
  5. The result is a fibrous plug crossed by several small channels — flow is partially restored but never normal.The original single wide lumen has been replaced by several narrow ones; resistance is permanently higher. Clinically this is why post-thrombotic syndrome (chronic leg swelling, pain, stasis ulcers) follows a large DVT even after successful treatment.
  6. Occasionally the organized thrombus undergoes dystrophic calcification, forming a hard nodule known as a phlebolith.Necrotic and fibrotic tissue with a high local calcium–phosphate product mineralises even when serum calcium is normal. Phleboliths are commonly seen incidentally in the pelvis on X-ray.

Summary — Thrombosis in three sentences
  1. Thrombus development depends on the relative contribution of the three components of Virchow's triad: endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow, and hypercoagulability.
  2. Thrombi may resolve, propagate, organize (with or without recanalization), calcify, or embolize.
  3. Thrombosis injures tissue in exactly two ways: by local vascular occlusion, or by distal embolisation.

Embolism

A thrombus is dangerous where it forms. An embolus is dangerous somewhere else entirely — and that displacement is the whole point. Ninety-nine percent of emboli are dislodged thrombi, but the remaining one percent (fat, air, nitrogen, amniotic fluid, tumour, cholesterol, marrow) produce some of the most distinctive syndromes in pathology.

By the end of this Part you should be able to

  • Trace an embolus from any starting point to its destination, including paradoxical and retrograde routes.
  • Explain the three mechanisms by which a large pulmonary embolus kills.
  • Compare thromboembolism, fat, gas and amniotic fluid embolism on setting, latent period, morphology and outcome.

4.1 Definition and the Four Routes of Travel

Definition

Embolism is the occlusion of part of the cardiovascular system by any detached intravascular mass carried there in the bloodstream from a distant site. The transported mass is an embolus; it may be solid, liquid or gaseous.

99% of emboli are dislodged thrombi — hence the term thromboembolism. Unless a vignette gives you a specific reason to think otherwise (long bone fracture, labour, diving, central line), assume thromboembolism.

High-Yield — the one rule that answers most embolism questions

Follow the blood. Ask where the embolus started, then trace the circulation forwards until the vessel becomes narrower than the embolus. Veins → right heart → lung. Left heart or aorta → systemic organs. If a venous-origin embolus reaches a systemic organ, there must be a shunt — that is a paradoxical embolus by definition.

4.2 Pulmonary Thromboembolism

Over 95% of pulmonary emboli originate in the deep veins of the lower limb, most often the popliteal, femoral and iliac veins. Note that calf vein thrombi are common but rarely embolise dangerously; it is proximal propagation above the knee that creates a clinically significant embolic risk. This is why ultrasound surveillance concentrates on the proximal veins.

Outcome depends on the size of the embolus

Large ("saddle") embolus — sudden death

Mechanism — the three ways a massive PE kills
  1. Mechanical obstruction of pulmonary outflow. Occlusion of the main trunk or of >60% of the pulmonary vascular bed abruptly prevents blood reaching the left heart. Left ventricular preload collapses, cardiac output falls, and the patient becomes profoundly hypotensive.The left ventricle can only pump what it receives. A completely obstructed pulmonary circuit means zero left-sided filling, which is electromechanical dissociation — the heart beats but ejects nothing.
  2. Acute right heart strain — acute cor pulmonale. Pulmonary vascular resistance rises abruptly, and the thin-walled right ventricle must suddenly generate far higher pressure.The right ventricle is built for a low-pressure circuit; it has no time to hypertrophy. It dilates acutely, its wall tension and oxygen demand rise while its coronary perfusion pressure falls, and it fails. The dilated RV also bows the interventricular septum leftwards, further impairing LV filling.
  3. Reflex vascular and bronchial spasm. Serotonin, thromboxane and other mediators released from platelets within the embolus cause pulmonary arterial vasoconstriction and bronchoconstriction.This amplifies the mechanical obstruction chemically. It explains why an embolus that occludes only a fraction of the bed can still cause severe haemodynamic compromise, and why hypoxaemia is often worse than the degree of occlusion predicts.
Sudden death, right heart failure, or cardiovascular collapse. Roughly two-thirds of fatal pulmonary emboli kill within the first hour.

Small emboli

Most pulmonary emboli are small and clinically silent: they occlude a peripheral branch, and the lung's dual blood supply — pulmonary artery plus bronchial artery — keeps the parenchyma alive while the embolus is lysed and organized. Such emboli are often found incidentally at autopsy.

But if the bronchial circulation is already inadequate — as in a patient with chronic passive congestion of the lung from left heart failure — the same small embolus produces pulmonary haemorrhage or a red (haemorrhagic) infarct. This is the direct link back to §1.5 and forward to §5.3, and it is a favourite examination connection.

Recurrent small emboli over months to years progressively obliterate the pulmonary vascular bed, producing chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular hypertrophy and chronic cor pulmonale.

Clinical Correlation — recognising and preventing PE

Presentation: sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, tachypnoea, tachycardia, and — if infarction has occurred — haemoptysis and a pleural rub. Hypotension and raised JVP indicate a massive embolus. The ECG classically (but uncommonly) shows S1Q3T3; sinus tachycardia is far more frequent. D-dimer is sensitive but not specific — useful to exclude PE in a low-probability patient. CT pulmonary angiography is the diagnostic standard.
Prevention is the real lesson. Because most PEs arise from leg DVT, and most DVTs arise from stasis during immobility, prophylaxis — early mobilisation, graduated compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression and low-molecular-weight heparin in at-risk inpatients — prevents far more deaths than treatment cures.

4.3 Systemic (Arterial) Thromboembolism

Origin: about 80% arise from intracardiac mural thrombi — two-thirds of these from left ventricular wall thrombus after myocardial infarction, and about a quarter from a dilated left atrium in atrial fibrillation. The remainder come from valvular vegetations, aortic aneurysms, ulcerated atherosclerotic plaques, and paradoxical emboli. In perhaps a third of cases no source is ever identified.

Destinations, in order of frequency: lower limbs (~75%), brain (~10%), then intestine, kidney and spleen.

Unlike pulmonary emboli, which lodge in a tissue with a dual blood supply, systemic emboli usually lodge in organs supplied by end arteries. The consequence is therefore infarction far more often than not — and this is the direct link into Part 5.

4.4 Fat and Marrow Embolism

Setting: fractures of the shafts of long bones (femur, tibia) — which have fatty marrow — and also soft tissue trauma, burns, orthopaedic procedures and liposuction. Fat and marrow enter the circulation through torn marrow sinusoids or ruptured adipose venules.

Crucially, fat embolism is common but fat embolism syndrome is rare. Fat globules can be found in the pulmonary circulation of up to 90% of patients with severe skeletal injury, yet fewer than 10% develop the clinical syndrome. The difference lies in the second, biochemical mechanism below.

Mechanism — the two-hit pathogenesis of fat embolism syndrome
  1. Hit 1 — mechanical. Fat globules and marrow fragments released from the fracture enter torn venules and travel to the lung, physically obstructing pulmonary capillaries.Marrow sinusoids are thin-walled and held open by the surrounding bone, so they cannot collapse; a fracture opens them directly into the venous circulation, and the intramedullary pressure generated by the injury forces marrow contents in.
  2. Hit 2 — biochemical. Over 24–72 hours, lipases hydrolyse the neutral fat into free fatty acids, which are directly toxic to endothelium and pneumocytes, and platelets aggregate on the globule surfaces.This is why there is a latent period. The mechanical obstruction alone is usually tolerable; the delayed chemical injury is what produces the syndrome. Free fatty acids damage the alveolar–capillary membrane in exactly the way that causes ARDS.
  3. Endothelial injury and platelet consumption produce diffuse alveolar damage, pulmonary edema, haemorrhage, atelectasis and thrombocytopenia.Platelets adhere to fat globules and are consumed — hence the petechial rash and the falling platelet count.
  4. Small globules deform and pass through the pulmonary capillary bed into the systemic circulation, reaching brain, kidney and skin.Unlike a rigid thrombus, a fat droplet is deformable and can squeeze through a capillary. This is why fat embolism causes cerebral signs without a shunt — a key distinction from thromboembolism.
The classic triad, 24–72 h after a long-bone fracture: ① respiratory distress (dyspnoea, tachypnoea, hypoxaemia), ② neurological abnormality (irritability, confusion, progressing to delirium or coma), ③ a petechial rash — characteristically over the upper anterior chest, axillae, neck and conjunctivae, and a valuable clue because it appears in no other embolic syndrome. Plus thrombocytopenia and anaemia. Mortality is around 10%. The older lecture terminology for the disseminated form is "lung–brain–kidney–skin embolism syndrome".

Quantitatively, the lecture notes that 9–20 g of fat entering the pulmonary circulation may occlude up to 75% of the pulmonary arteries, producing pulmonary edema, haemorrhage, atelectasis, asphyxia and right heart failure.

4.5 Gas Embolism and Decompression Sickness

Air embolism

How air gets in: chest trauma; cardiothoracic, neurosurgical (especially sitting-position operations) and obstetric procedures; insertion, use or removal of a central venous catheter; haemodialysis; laparoscopic insufflation; and rupture of uterine or vaginal veins during delivery or criminal abortion.

Mechanism — why gas is more dangerous than an equivalent volume of solid
  1. Air enters a vein where local venous pressure is below atmospheric — most importantly in neck and thoracic veins during inspiration.Inspiration generates negative intrathoracic pressure, which sucks air in through any open venous channel. This is why a patient having a central line removed is asked to hold their breath or lie head-down.
  2. Bubbles are carried to the right ventricle, where the beating heart churns air with blood into a compressible, frothy foam.This is the critical step. The right ventricle is a pump designed for incompressible fluid. Foam compresses instead of being ejected — the ventricle contracts but nothing leaves. This is called an air lock.
  3. Outflow from the right heart ceases; cardiac output collapses.Same end-point as a massive saddle embolus, reached by a different route.
  4. Bubbles also mechanically injure endothelium and activate platelets and complement at the blood–gas interface.A gas–liquid interface denatures plasma proteins, which is why gas emboli cause more inflammatory injury than their volume alone suggests.
Volume thresholds: small volumes are absorbed harmlessly; >100 mL causes acute distress; >300 mL entering rapidly is generally fatal. Management: place the patient in the left lateral decubitus, head-down (Durant) position so that air is trapped at the apex of the right ventricle away from the outflow tract, give 100% oxygen, and aspirate air from a central line if one is in place.

Decompression sickness (caisson disease)

Who: deep-sea divers and underwater construction workers ascending too rapidly from high ambient pressure to normal; and aviators or high-altitude pilots ascending from normal to low pressure.

Mechanism — why nitrogen, and why only on ascent
  1. At depth, ambient pressure is high, so by Henry's law far more gas dissolves in blood and tissue than at the surface.The solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to its partial pressure. At 30 m the ambient pressure is four atmospheres, so four times as much gas dissolves.
  2. Oxygen and CO2 are metabolised or buffered, but nitrogen is inert and highly fat-soluble, so it simply accumulates — preferentially in adipose tissue, bone marrow, nerve myelin and joint tissues.Nitrogen cannot be consumed by any metabolic pathway, so its only exit is back through the lung. It concentrates in fat because it is about five times more soluble in lipid than in water.
  3. On rapid ascent, ambient pressure falls faster than nitrogen can diffuse back into the lungs, so the dissolved gas comes out of solution as bubbles within blood, tissue fluid and fat.Exactly like opening a carbonated drink. Slow ascent — or a decompression stop — allows nitrogen to be exhaled gradually and no bubbles form. This is why the rate of ascent, not the depth alone, determines the disease.
  4. Bubbles obstruct capillaries, distend tissues and activate platelets and complement at the gas–blood interface.The same interfacial injury as in air embolism, but generated throughout the body simultaneously rather than at one entry point.
Acute — "the bends": severe pain in joints and muscles from bubbles in periarticular tissue, forcing the sufferer to bend over. "The chokes": respiratory distress from pulmonary vascular bubbles causing edema, haemorrhage and atelectasis. "The staggers": vertigo and neurological deficits from bubbles in the CNS. Chronic (caisson disease proper): repeated or persistent bubbles cause ischaemic necrosis of bone — avascular necrosis of the femoral head, humeral head and tibia — because these sites have fatty marrow and an end-arterial supply. Treatment is immediate recompression in a hyperbaric chamber followed by slow, controlled decompression, plus 100% oxygen.

4.6 Amniotic Fluid Embolism

Rare (about 1 in 40,000 deliveries) but catastrophic, with a mortality approaching 80% and accounting for roughly 10% of maternal deaths in developed countries. Survivors frequently have permanent neurological deficit.

Mechanism
  1. After rupture of the membranes, vigorous uterine contractions force amniotic fluid between the membranes and the uterine wall, up to the placental margin, and into the torn uterine venous sinusoids.Two conditions must coexist: a breach in the fetal membranes (providing access) and a pressure gradient driving fluid into an open maternal vein (provided by contraction against a torn placental bed).
  2. Amniotic fluid contents — fetal squames, lanugo hairs, vernix caseosa fat, mucin and meconium — travel to the pulmonary circulation.The uterine veins drain into the systemic venous system, so the lung is the first capillary bed reached.
  3. These particulates cause mechanical obstruction, but far more importantly they trigger an anaphylactoid reaction to fetal antigens, with massive release of vasoactive and procoagulant mediators.The amount of particulate matter is too small to account for the clinical severity. The dominant injury is immunological — which is why the condition is now often called anaphylactoid syndrome of pregnancy.
  4. Result: intense pulmonary vasoconstriction, right then left heart failure, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema (diffuse alveolar damage), and — because amniotic fluid is rich in tissue factor — fulminant DIC.The tissue factor content of amniotic fluid is what converts a cardiorespiratory catastrophe into a haemorrhagic one; the patient collapses and then bleeds uncontrollably from the uterus and every puncture site.
Sudden, during or immediately after labour: severe dyspnoea, cyanosis, hypotensive shock, seizures, coma, then profuse haemorrhage from DIC. Autopsy finding (classic and examinable): fetal squamous cells, lanugo hairs, vernix fat and mucin within pulmonary arteries and capillaries.

4.7 Other Emboli

  • Tumour embolism — clusters of malignant cells entering the circulation. This is the mechanism of haematogenous metastasis. Renal cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma characteristically grow as solid tumour thrombus along the renal vein and IVC.
  • Septic embolism — an infected thrombus fragment, typically from infective endocarditis. Produces abscesses at the site of lodgement rather than a bland infarct, and may weaken the arterial wall to form a mycotic aneurysm. See §5.5.
  • Atherosclerotic (cholesterol) embolism — fragments of a ruptured plaque, recognisable microscopically by needle-shaped cholesterol clefts within the occluded vessel. Often follows angiography or vascular surgery; produces blue toe syndrome, livedo reticularis and renal impairment.
  • Bone marrow embolism — intact fragments of haematopoietic marrow with fat, found in pulmonary vessels after fracture or vigorous cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Usually an incidental autopsy finding.
  • Foreign body embolism — catheter fragments, intravenous drug-user talc or cotton fibres, bullet or shrapnel fragments.

Infarction

This is where the story ends: tissue that has been cut off from its blood supply and died. Nearly every clinically important consequence of the previous four Parts — myocardial infarction, stroke, bowel infarction, gangrene — arrives here. The two questions worth asking of any infarct are: why did it happen at all (many occlusions cause none), and why is it that colour.

By the end of this Part you should be able to

  • List the four factors that determine whether a vascular occlusion produces an infarct.
  • Explain mechanistically why some infarcts are white and others red.
  • Describe the gross and microscopic evolution of an infarct over hours, days and weeks.
  • Differentiate dry, wet and gas gangrene.
  • Trace a complete thrombosis → embolism → infarction sequence in a single patient.

5.1 Definition and Causes

Definition

An infarct is an area of ischaemic necrosis of a tissue or organ caused by occlusion of either its arterial supply or its venous drainage. The process of its formation is infarction.

Note the "or". Most infarcts are arterial, but venous occlusion can also cause one — see §5.3, where it is the key to understanding the red infarct.

Causes, in order of frequency

  • Thrombotic arterial occlusion — usually on a ruptured atherosclerotic plaque. The commonest cause overall; this is myocardial infarction and most strokes.
  • Arterial embolism — from cardiac mural thrombus, valvular vegetation, aortic plaque or a paradoxical venous embolus.
  • Vasospasm — cocaine-induced coronary spasm, Prinzmetal angina.
  • Extrinsic compression of a vessel — tumour, volvulus, intussusception, incarcerated hernia, or a testicular/ovarian torsion. Note that twisting compresses the thin-walled veins first, which is why these lesions produce haemorrhagic infarcts.
  • Vessel wall expansion or dissection — atheromatous plaque haemorrhage, aortic dissection occluding a branch ostium.
  • Venous thrombosis — where there is no alternative outflow, e.g. ovarian, testicular or mesenteric vein thrombosis, or superior sagittal sinus thrombosis.
  • Vasculitis, sickle cell crisis, and external pressure (a tight cast, prolonged pressure causing a pressure sore).

5.2 The Four Determinants of Whether an Infarct Develops

Occlusion does not automatically mean infarction. A surgeon can tie off the splenic artery and the spleen may survive on short gastric collaterals; a pulmonary artery branch can be blocked and the lung remains viable. Four variables decide the outcome.

5.3 White versus Red Infarct

White (pale, anaemic) infarct

Mechanism — why some infarcts turn pale
  1. An end artery supplying a solid, compact organ is occluded. The organ has little or no effective collateral circulation.Kidney, spleen, heart and retina are supplied by end arteries with minimal anastomoses, so occlusion removes the only supply to that territory.
  2. Inflow stops completely. Only the small amount of blood already inside the territory remains.There is no second route to push more blood in — this is the decisive difference from a dual-supply organ.
  3. The tissue undergoes coagulative necrosis: cells die but their structural outlines persist for days.Ischaemia denatures both the structural proteins and the lysosomal hydrolases that would otherwise digest the cell, so the architecture is preserved as a "ghost" outline. The brain is the sole exception — see the trap box below.
  4. The red cells trapped in the territory are lysed, and their haemoglobin is degraded and removed by macrophages.Dead red cells have no way to maintain their membranes; haemolysis follows, and phagocytes clear the pigment. Nothing new arrives to replace it, because inflow is blocked.
  5. The necrotic zone therefore progressively decolorises, becoming pale yellow-white and sharply demarcated from the surrounding congested viable tissue.A tissue is red because of haemoglobin. Remove the haemoglobin and prevent replacement, and what is left is the pale colour of the protein matrix itself.
A pale, firm, wedge-shaped infarct with the apex at the occluded vessel and the base at the organ surface, often with a thin hyperaemic rim of inflamed viable tissue at its edge. Typical organs: kidney, spleen, heart.

Red (haemorrhagic) infarct

Mechanism — the three routes to a red infarct
  1. Route A — venous occlusion. Outflow is blocked while arterial inflow continues. Blood is pumped in but cannot leave, so the tissue engorges. Rising intravascular pressure eventually stops arterial inflow too, and the tissue dies — but it dies full of blood.This is the central insight. A venous infarct is haemorrhagic by definition, because the blood was driven in under arterial pressure before the tissue died, and had no way out. Classic examples: testicular and ovarian torsion, mesenteric vein thrombosis, volvulus, intussusception, strangulated hernia.
  2. Route B — a loose, spongy tissue. Lung and bowel have abundant interstitial space into which blood can pool.A compact organ like the kidney has no room for blood to accumulate; a spongy organ like the lung does. The same volume of haemorrhage disappears in one and is conspicuous in the other.
  3. Route C — dual circulation or collateral reperfusion. After the main artery is occluded, blood still enters the dead zone from the second supply or from an adjacent bed — but not enough to keep it alive.In the lung, the bronchial arteries continue to perfuse a territory whose pulmonary artery is blocked. That flow is sufficient to fill the necrotic zone with blood but not sufficient to oxygenate it, because bronchial arterial flow is only ~1% of cardiac output. Haemorrhage is compounded by ischaemic damage to the capillary walls, which then leak. The same happens after reperfusion — for example thrombolysis or angioplasty restoring flow to an already-necrotic myocardium, which converts a white infarct into a haemorrhagic one.
A dark red to purple-black, firm, wedge-shaped infarct, often with an overlying fibrinous exudate on the serosal surface. Typical organs: lung, small intestine, testis, ovary — and any organ after reperfusion. As the extravasated red cells are broken down over days, the lesion becomes brown from haemosiderin.

Exam Trap — the brain is different, twice over

First, cerebral infarcts undergo liquefactive, not coagulative, necrosis. The reason is mechanistic: the brain has very little supporting collagenous stroma and a very high lipid content, and microglia release abundant hydrolytic enzymes, so the dead tissue digests itself into a fluid-filled cyst rather than retaining its outline. Second, the brain is on the vulnerable list for hypoxia (neurons die in 3–4 minutes) despite having the circle of Willis — because that anastomosis protects only against proximal occlusion, not against occlusion of the small penetrating end arteries beyond it.

5.5 Septic Infarct

When an infarct is caused by an infected (septic) embolus — classically from infective endocarditis — or when bacteria seed a bland infarct, the lesion does not heal by scarring. Instead it is converted into an abscess.

Why: necrotic tissue is an ideal culture medium — it is protein-rich, has no blood supply to deliver leukocytes, antibodies or antibiotics, and no mechanism to clear organisms. Bacteria therefore multiply unchecked, drawing in a massive neutrophil response that liquefies the tissue and walls it off with a pyogenic membrane. The inflammatory response is far more intense than in a bland infarct, and healing requires drainage rather than simple organization.

Additional consequence: organisms in the embolus may destroy the arterial wall at the lodging site, producing a mycotic aneurysm that can rupture catastrophically — a classic cause of fatal intracerebral haemorrhage in endocarditis.

5.7 Fate of an Infarct, and the Complete Chain

Fate

  • Small infarct — softens, is digested by macrophages and completely absorbed; may leave no trace.
  • Large infarct — undergoes organization: granulation tissue replaces necrotic tissue and matures into a contracted fibrous scar. The organ surface becomes depressed over the scar.
  • Septic infarct — becomes an abscess, requiring drainage.
  • Infarction of brain, myocardium or lung — may cause sudden death, from arrhythmia, herniation or acute right heart failure respectively.
  • Brain infarct specifically — liquefies and leaves a fluid-filled cystic cavity bounded by reactive gliosis, not a collagenous scar.

The complete chain — one patient, five sections

Worked example

A 68-year-old woman undergoes total hip replacement. She is immobile for four days post-operatively.

  1. Thrombosis (Part 3). Immobility → stasis in the calf veins; surgery → massive tissue factor release and an acute-phase rise in fibrinogen → hypercoagulability; the operation itself injures pelvic vein endothelium. All three arms of Virchow's triad are satisfied simultaneously, and a red (stasis) thrombus forms in the femoral vein, growing antegradely with a poorly anchored tail.
  2. Congestion and edema (Part 1). The occluded femoral vein raises capillary hydrostatic pressure upstream. Her leg becomes swollen, cyanotic, cool and painful — passive congestion plus pitting edema.
  3. Embolism (Part 4). On day 6 she sits up. The tail detaches, travels via the iliac vein, IVC and right heart, and lodges in a segmental branch of the right pulmonary artery — a venous thromboembolism to the lung.
  4. Infarction (Part 5). She has long-standing left heart failure, so her bronchial circulation is already compromised by chronic passive pulmonary congestion (Part 1 again). The dual supply is therefore inadequate, and a red (haemorrhagic) wedge infarct forms — spongy tissue, dual supply, incomplete reperfusion.
  5. Hemorrhage (Part 2). The infarcted lung bleeds into the alveoli, and she coughs up blood — haemoptysis. Fibrinous exudate over the infarcted segment irritates the pleura, producing pleuritic chest pain and an audible rub.
Summary — Infarction in three sentences
  1. Infarcts are areas of ischaemic, usually coagulative, necrosis caused by occlusion of arterial supply or, less commonly, venous drainage.
  2. They are most commonly caused by occlusive arterial thrombi or by embolisation of arterial or venous thrombi.
  3. Infarcts caused by venous occlusion, or occurring in loose tissues with a dual blood supply, are typically haemorrhagic (red); those caused by arterial occlusion in compact end-arterial tissues are pale (white).

6.2 Answer Key with Full Explanations

Q1 — CAlveolar macrophages that have phagocytosed extravasated red cells. Chronic left heart failure raises left atrial and hence pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure. The over-distended, 0.5 µm-thick septal capillaries rupture in small numbers, spilling red cells into the alveoli. Alveolar macrophages phagocytose them; haemoglobin is degraded, but the liberated iron cannot be excreted and is stored as haemosiderin — the coarse golden-brown, Prussian-blue-positive granules. These are heart failure cells (siderophages), and they are why the sputum is rust-coloured. D Surfactant is not pigmented and does not stain with Prussian blue. E Myeloperoxidase produces no visible pigment and the cells described are mononuclear, not neutrophils. A Anthracotic carbon pigment is black and Prussian-blue negative, because it contains no iron — this is the key discriminator. B Keratin is not granular, not brown, and not iron-containing. See §1.5.
Q2 — CCentrilobular (zone 3). Two independent facts converge. First, blood leaves the lobule through the central vein, so back-pressure from a failing right heart is transmitted to the central vein and perivenular sinusoids before anywhere else. Second, blood enters at the portal tracts and gives up oxygen as it travels inward, so zone 3 sees the lowest pO₂ and is the first to die when flow slows. Congestion and hypoxic necrosis therefore coincide in zone 3, producing the dark red foci; the surviving, often fatty periportal hepatocytes give the pale tan background. B Zone 1 receives oxygenated blood first and is the protected zone. D Zone 2 is intermediate, not extreme. E Portal tract fibrosis is the pattern of biliary disease, not cardiac congestion. A The lesion is strictly zonal — that is precisely what creates the regular nutmeg mottling. See §1.6.
Q3 — EDecreased plasma colloid osmotic pressure. Massive proteinuria with fatty casts and severe hypoalbuminaemia, without haematuria, defines nephrotic syndrome. Albumin generates almost all of the ~25 mmHg oncotic pressure that holds water inside the capillary; at 1.8 g/dL that inward pull can no longer balance even a normal hydrostatic pressure, so fluid leaves the circulation everywhere. The periorbital distribution and the worsening on waking reflect the extreme looseness of periorbital tissue and the absence of gravity dependence. D Would give dependent edema (legs, sacrum) and is the mechanism of heart failure. A Would produce a protein-rich exudate; here the vessel wall is intact and the fluid is a transudate. B Rising interstitial pressure opposes edema — it is the natural brake. C Would cause localised, non-pitting, protein-rich swelling, not generalised periorbital edema. See §1.7–1.8.
Q4 — AProtein-rich fluid that has provoked interstitial fibrosis. Axillary node clearance severs the lymphatic channels that normally return escaped plasma protein to the blood. Protein accumulates in the interstitium, raising interstitial oncotic pressure and drawing out still more water — a self-perpetuating loop. Protein-rich fluid is viscous and displaces poorly under a finger; over years it also stimulates collagen deposition, producing brawny, woody induration. Both effects make the swelling non-pitting. B Low specific gravity describes a transudate, which pits readily. C A neutrophil-rich fluid would be an inflammatory exudate — there is no infection here. D Anatomical location does not determine pitting. E Light's criteria classify effusions; they say nothing about pitting and would in any case describe a transudate. See §1.8.
Q5 — ECongestive heart failure. Every number points to a transudate: fluid/serum protein 0.25 (<0.5), fluid/serum LDH 0.32 (<0.6), specific gravity 1.008 (<1.012), few cells, and no clotting because fibrinogen has not escaped. A transudate means the vessel wall is intact and the problem is a Starling-force imbalance — so look outside the pleura, at the heart, liver or kidney. Bilateral effusions strongly favour a systemic cause. B, C, D, E all produce exudates (increased permeability from inflammation or tumour), with protein >3 g/dL, high LDH, high cellularity, turbid fluid and clotting on standing. A pulmonary embolus with infarction also typically gives a unilateral, often blood-stained exudate. See §1.9.
Q6 — BActive hyperemia. Accumulating metabolites (adenosine, CO₂, H⁺, K⁺) relax arteriolar smooth muscle, dropping resistance and increasing inflow. The bed fills with blood arriving directly from the arterial side, which is fully oxygenated — hence erythema — and the high volume of warm core blood delivered per minute raises surface temperature. C Congestion is blue and cool because the blood is deoxygenated and flow is reduced; that is the opposite of what is described. D There is no inflammation and no protein-rich exudate. E Haemoconcentration does not cause erythema and warmth. A Reactive hyperemia specifically follows release of an occlusion; here the stimulus is ongoing metabolic demand, not prior ischaemia. See §1.2.
Q7 — AAnterior cerebral artery. In subfalcine (cingulate) herniation the cingulate gyrus is forced beneath the free edge of the falx cerebri. The anterior cerebral artery runs in exactly that location, along the medial surface of the hemisphere beside the falx, so it is compressed between the herniating gyrus and the rigid dural edge — producing infarction of the medial frontal and parietal cortex (contralateral leg weakness). B The posterior cerebral artery is compressed in uncal (transtentorial) herniation, against the tentorial edge. C The basilar artery is involved in central/tonsillar herniation with Duret haemorrhages. D The middle meningeal artery is torn in extradural haematoma after temporal bone fracture — a cause, not a consequence, of raised pressure. E Sagittal sinus thrombosis causes venous infarction, but it is not compressed by subfalcine herniation. See §1.10.
Q8 — DFactor VIII deficiency (haemophilia A). The pattern is textbook for a secondary hemostasis defect: deep bleeding into a joint (haemarthrosis), and delayed rebleeding hours after initial haemostasis was achieved. The explanation is mechanistic — the platelet plug forms normally (hence normal platelet count and bleeding time, and initial haemostasis) but is never reinforced by fibrin, so it disintegrates hours later. Factor VIII is in the intrinsic pathway, so PTT is prolonged and PT normal. E von Willebrand disease impairs platelet adhesion, so it causes mucocutaneous bleeding with a prolonged bleeding time — both absent here (though PTT can be prolonged because vWF carries factor VIII). A Factor VII is extrinsic, so it would prolong PT, not PTT. B Glanzmann thrombasthenia is a platelet aggregation defect — mucocutaneous bleeding, prolonged bleeding time, normal PTT. C Scurvy causes perifollicular haemorrhage and bleeding gums with normal coagulation studies. See §2.2.
Q9 — CThe fibrous parietal pericardium cannot stretch acutely. This is the purest illustration of site overriding volume. 250 mL is only about 5% of blood volume — trivial as blood loss. But it is confined within a non-compliant fibrous sac, so intrapericardial pressure rises steeply, exceeds right atrial and ventricular diastolic pressure, and prevents the heart filling. The result is obstructive shock with Beck's triad: hypotension, distended neck veins and muffled heart sounds. Free wall rupture is a classic day 3–7 complication of transmural infarction, when macrophage digestion has weakened the wall most. D 250 mL is ~5%, not >20%. E Haemolysis of that volume would not cause acute collapse. A Coronary ostia arise inside the aortic root, not in the pericardial space. B A vasovagal reflex causes bradycardia and does not produce raised JVP. See §2.3, §2.5.
Q10 — EClass II haemorrhage (15–30%), and the normal haemoglobin is uninformative. Tachycardia with a narrowed pulse pressure (118/96 = 22 mmHg) is the signature of class II: sympathetic vasoconstriction raises diastolic pressure and maintains systolic pressure, so blood pressure looks deceptively normal. In acute haemorrhage, whole blood is lost — red cells and plasma in the same proportion — so the concentration of haemoglobin is initially unchanged; it falls only over hours as interstitial fluid shifts in to refill the vascular space. A Class I loss produces essentially no signs; he has marked tachycardia and peripheral shutdown. B This is the classic trap and is factually wrong, for the reason just given. C Class IV would show profound hypotension and obtundation. D A narrowed pulse pressure indicates vasoconstriction; septic shock classically gives a widened pulse pressure with warm peripheries. See §2.5.
Q11 — AHaem oxygenase producing biliverdin. Inside the macrophage phagolysosome, haem oxygenase opens the porphyrin ring, releasing iron and carbon monoxide and yielding biliverdin, which is green. This is the rate-limiting step of haem degradation and the source of the green phase of a bruise. B Biliverdin reductase produces bilirubin, which is yellow — that is the next phase (day 7–10). C Haemosiderin is golden-brown and is the last phase. D Conjugation occurs in the liver, not in the bruise, and does not change the colour seen in skin. E Myeloperoxidase gives pus its green tint, but that is a neutrophil product in infection, not a feature of resolving haemorrhage. See §2.4.
Q12 — BAll three arms of Virchow's triad act simultaneously. Immobility abolishes the calf muscle pump, producing venous stasis; major surgery releases large amounts of tissue factor and provokes an acute-phase rise in fibrinogen and factor VIII, producing hypercoagulability; and pelvic and femoral vein manipulation during hip replacement causes direct endothelial injury. This convergence is why orthopaedic surgery carries the highest DVT risk of any procedure and why prophylactic anticoagulation is routine. C Turbulence is an arterial phenomenon; veins have slow, low-pressure flow where stasis, not turbulence, dominates. D and E cause edema, not thrombosis. A vWF deficiency causes bleeding, the opposite problem. See §3.6.
Q13 — DIt formed in flowing blood before death. The laminations are lines of Zahn — alternating pale layers of platelets and fibrin with dark red-cell-rich layers. They can only form where blood is moving, because the alternation is produced by variation in flow during the cardiac cycle: platelets deposit preferentially during rapid flow, red cells are trapped when flow momentarily slows. Their presence therefore proves an ante-mortem thrombus. The dry, dull, friable texture confirms it. E A post-mortem clot forms by gravity sedimentation and shows two layers ("chicken fat" over "red currant jelly"), not fine alternating laminations; it is also wet, shiny, gelatinous and non-adherent. A A myxoma is a gelatinous tumour mass with a stalk, not a laminated clot. B Fibrin microthrombi of DIC are microscopic only and occlude capillaries. C Friability is a feature of thrombus, not post-mortem clot — this option inverts the discriminator. See §3.8, §3.13.
Q14 — CFactor V Leiden. A point mutation substitutes glutamine for arginine at position 506 of factor V — the exact residue at which activated protein C cleaves and inactivates factor Va. The mutant factor Va is therefore resistant to cleavage and continues to support thrombin generation. The laboratory phenotype described, "failure of activated protein C to prolong the clotting time", is literally the APC-resistance assay. It is the commonest inherited thrombophilia, and the combined oral contraceptive multiplies the risk many-fold. D Antithrombin III deficiency presents with apparent heparin resistance, not APC resistance. E The prothrombin G20210A mutation raises prothrombin levels; APC function is normal. A HIT is an acquired, heparin-dependent antibody disorder with thrombocytopenia. B Protein S deficiency reduces APC's cofactor, so adding exogenous APC would still work — the assay result described points specifically to a defect in the substrate, factor V. See §3.7.
Q15 — EIgG against heparin–platelet factor 4 complexes. Heparin binds platelet factor 4, creating a neoantigen. IgG against this complex binds the platelet surface and cross-links FcγRIIA receptors, which activates the platelet. Activated platelets are cleared from the circulation (thrombocytopenia) and simultaneously drive thrombin generation (thrombosis). This is the paradox of HIT: a falling platelet count with clotting, not bleeding. Timing (day 5–10 of heparin) and a >50% fall are the clinical clues. Management: stop all heparin and start a direct thrombin inhibitor — warfarin alone is contraindicated acutely because it can precipitate venous limb gangrene. A Marrow suppression would not cause thrombosis. B Heparin does not release tissue factor; and DIC would prolong PT and PTT and consume fibrinogen. C Antithrombin is not consumed in this way. D Dilution would occur on day 0–1, not day 6, and would not cause limb ischaemia. See §3.7.
Q16 — AWidespread microvascular fibrin thrombi consuming platelets and clotting factors — DIC. Placental abruption releases massive amounts of tissue factor, activating coagulation throughout the circulation at once. Fibrin microthrombi form in capillaries everywhere, causing organ ischaemia and shearing red cells into schistocytes as they pass through fibrin strands. Manufacturing that fibrin exhausts platelets and clotting factors, and secondary fibrinolysis floods the plasma with fibrin degradation products that further inhibit haemostasis — hence bleeding. The laboratory panel (low platelets, prolonged PT and PTT, low fibrinogen, very high D-dimer, schistocytes) is diagnostic. B Isolated factor VIII deficiency prolongs only PTT and does not lower platelets or fibrinogen. C ITP gives isolated thrombocytopenia with normal PT, PTT and fibrinogen. D Vitamin K deficiency prolongs PT then PTT but leaves platelets, fibrinogen and D-dimer normal. E Pregnancy raises fibrinogen; here it is consumed and low, which is the point. See §3.11.
Q17 — BOrganization with recanalization. Over days, leukocytes infiltrate the thrombus and endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts grow in from the adjacent wall — recruited by PDGF and TGF-β released from platelets within the thrombus itself. Collagen is deposited and the thrombus becomes granulation tissue and then scar (organization). Capillary sprouts within it elongate and anastomose into channels that eventually reconnect the two ends of the lumen (recanalization). One wide lumen is thereby replaced by several narrow ones, so resistance is permanently higher — the anatomical basis of post-thrombotic syndrome: chronic swelling, pain and venous stasis ulceration. C Complete lysis would leave a normal patent vein. D Propagation is enlargement, not fibrous replacement. E Embolisation removes the thrombus to a distant site. A Calcification alone produces a phlebolith, not multiple perfused channels. See §3.12.
Q18 — DThrombomodulin binds thrombin and converts it into an activator of protein C. This is one of the most elegant switches in physiology: thrombin, the most powerful procoagulant enzyme in the body, becomes an anticoagulant the moment it binds thrombomodulin on intact endothelium. The complex activates protein C, which with protein S inactivates factors Va and VIIIa. This is how a clot is confined to the injured segment — the surrounding healthy endothelium actively switches off any thrombin that drifts onto it. E vWF promotes platelet adhesion by bridging collagen to GpIb. A PAI inhibits fibrinolysis; t-PA degrades fibrin. B Tissue factor initiates the extrinsic cascade; heparin-like molecules accelerate antithrombin III. C TXA₂ promotes aggregation; prostacyclin (PGI₂) inhibits it. See §3.2, §3.5.
Q19 — CLibman–Sacks endocarditis. Three features together are specific: the patient has SLE, the vegetations are sterile and non-destructive, and they sit on both surfaces of the leaflet. The pathogenesis is deposition of immune complexes and complement along the endocardium with secondary platelet aggregation, and it is strongly associated with antiphospholipid antibodies. D Infective endocarditis produces large, bulky, destructive vegetations containing organisms, and can perforate a cusp. E Rheumatic valvulitis gives small vegetations along the line of closure only, with Aschoff bodies, and heals with commissural fusion and mitral stenosis. A Marantic endocarditis is also sterile and bland, but occurs on the line of closure in patients with advanced malignancy or DIC, not SLE, and is not verrucous on both surfaces. B Carcinoid disease causes fibrous plaque-like thickening of right-sided valves. See §3.10.
Q20 — ESudan-positive fat globules on a frozen section. The vignette gives the complete fat embolism syndrome: long-bone fracture, a 24–72 hour latent period, respiratory distress, neurological change, thrombocytopenia, and the highly specific petechial rash over the upper chest and axillae. The latency is the diagnostic clue and is mechanistic — the mechanical obstruction alone is tolerable, but over 1–3 days lipases hydrolyse neutral fat into free fatty acids that are directly toxic to the alveolar–capillary membrane. Fat must be demonstrated on a frozen section with a lipid stain, because routine paraffin processing dissolves it in xylene, leaving only empty vacuoles. A Lines of Zahn indicate a thromboembolus; that would present suddenly, with no latency and no petechial rash. B Squames and lanugo are pathognomonic of amniotic fluid embolism. C Frothy right-ventricular blood is the finding in air embolism. D Cholesterol clefts indicate atheroembolism, which follows vascular instrumentation. See §4.4.
Q21 — AAcross the septal defect — a paradoxical embolus. Follow the blood. A thrombus in a leg vein must travel to the right heart. Normally it would then be pumped into the pulmonary arteries and be filtered there. In the presence of a patent foramen ovale, a transient rise in right atrial pressure (straining, coughing, or the pressure rise caused by the embolus itself) opens the flap and allows the embolus to cross into the left atrium, bypassing the pulmonary filter and entering the systemic circulation. Whenever a venous-origin embolus reaches a systemic organ, a shunt must exist. B A pulmonary artery embolus cannot reach the brain; the pulmonary capillary bed is the end of that route. C and E are plausible stroke mechanisms in general but are excluded here by her age, the absence of cardiac or carotid disease, and the documented DVT and PFO. D Batson's plexus explains retrograde metastasis to vertebrae, not a hemispheric stroke. See §4.1.
Q22 — BFetal squamous cells, lanugo hairs and mucin in pulmonary vessels. These are the pathognomonic autopsy findings of amniotic fluid embolism, along with vernix caseosa fat. The clinical sequence is characteristic and follows the mechanism: particulate matter and — far more importantly — fetal antigens trigger an anaphylactoid reaction causing pulmonary vasoconstriction, cardiorespiratory collapse and seizures; then the high tissue factor content of amniotic fluid precipitates fulminant DIC, which is why catastrophic haemorrhage follows the collapse. C A saddle thromboembolus would cause sudden death but not seizures followed by DIC-driven bleeding. D Fat embolism requires a fracture and has a 24–72 h latency. E Nitrogen bubbles require decompression. A Marrow emboli follow fracture or CPR and are usually incidental. See §4.6.
Q23 — DAir churned into compressible foam — an air lock. During inspiration, intrathoracic venous pressure falls below atmospheric, so removing a central line can suck air into the vein. The bubbles reach the right ventricle, where the beating heart whips air and blood into foam. A ventricle is a pump designed for an incompressible fluid: foam simply compresses under systole instead of being ejected, so forward output ceases and the patient collapses. The churning also generates the classic "mill-wheel" murmur. Management: left lateral decubitus, head-down (Durant position), 100% oxygen, and aspiration through the line. E That is decompression sickness, which requires a change in ambient pressure. A That is the second hit of fat embolism syndrome. B Catheters do not release tissue factor in amounts causing instantaneous DIC. C A vagal response causes bradycardia and would not produce a mill-wheel murmur. See §4.5.
Q24 — CNitrogen. At depth, high ambient pressure forces far more gas into solution (Henry's law). Oxygen is metabolised and CO₂ is buffered and excreted, but nitrogen is metabolically inert — no pathway consumes it — and it is about five times more soluble in lipid than in water, so it accumulates preferentially in fat, marrow, myelin and joint tissues. On rapid ascent, ambient pressure falls faster than nitrogen can diffuse back to the lungs, and it comes out of solution as bubbles: joint pain (bends), respiratory distress (chokes) and neurological signs (staggers). Chronically, bubbles in the fatty marrow of end-arterial bone cause avascular necrosis of the femoral and humeral heads — exactly the late finding described. D and E are metabolically handled and do not accumulate. A Helium is used deliberately in deep mixes precisely because it is less soluble in lipid and less narcotic. B Carbon monoxide causes hypoxic injury, not bubbles. See §4.5.
Q25 — EThe kidney is a compact end-arterial organ; the lung is spongy with a dual supply. In the kidney, an embolus removes the only supply to that territory. No more blood can enter, the trapped red cells are lysed and their pigment cleared by macrophages, and — because nothing replaces them — the necrotic zone progressively decolorises to pale yellow-white. In the lung, the bronchial arteries continue to perfuse the territory whose pulmonary artery is blocked. That flow is enough to fill the dead zone with blood but not enough to oxygenate it (bronchial flow is ~1% of cardiac output), and ischaemically damaged capillaries leak — so the infarct is haemorrhagic. The spongy interstitium gives the blood somewhere to pool. A Both undergo coagulative necrosis; only the brain liquefies. B Nothing in the vignette suggests infection, and septic infarcts become abscesses regardless of organ. C Age changes red to brown, not white to red, and the vignette gives no timeline difference. D Renal tubular epithelium is in fact highly susceptible to hypoxia. See §5.3.
Q26 — AVenous outflow was obstructed first while arterial inflow continued. Intussusception (like volvulus, strangulated hernia and testicular torsion) compresses the mesenteric vessels. The thin-walled, low-pressure veins collapse first; the thicker, higher-pressure arteries keep pumping blood in. Blood is therefore driven into a segment that cannot drain, so the bowel engorges and becomes dark purple-black. Only when intravascular pressure finally equals arterial pressure does inflow stop and the tissue die — full of blood. That is a haemorrhagic (red) infarct. The wetness and foul smell indicate superimposed bacterial overgrowth from the gut lumen, making this wet gangrene, which is why there is no sharp demarcation and why it is a surgical emergency. B Pure arterial occlusion in a drained tissue produces dry, pale, sharply demarcated gangrene. C Caseous necrosis is a feature of tuberculosis. D Chylous accumulation is milky lymph, not black blood. E Clostridial gas gangrene affects deep contaminated muscle wounds and produces crepitus. See §5.3, §5.6.
Q27 — BMacrophage-rich tissue digesting necrotic myocytes, with collagen not yet deposited. Days 3–7 after infarction is the moment of maximum mechanical weakness, and the reason is a timing gap: neutrophils and macrophages have removed the dead myocytes and their supporting matrix, but fibroblasts have not yet laid down enough collagen to replace them. The wall is therefore at its thinnest and softest just as normal systolic pressure continues to load it. Hence the classic day 3–7 complications: free wall rupture (tamponade), septal rupture (VSD) and papillary muscle rupture (acute mitral regurgitation). C A dense collagenous scar takes 2–8 weeks and is strong; rupture at that stage is rare. D Contraction band necrosis is a reperfusion finding at the infarct margin and does not explain rupture. E Granulomatous inflammation is not part of infarct healing. A Cardiac myocytes are permanent cells and do not regenerate. See §5.4.
Q28 — DIn a normal lung the bronchial supply is adequate; chronic congestion compromises it. This question integrates the whole session. The lung normally resists infarction because it has a dual blood supply: when a pulmonary arterial branch is blocked, the bronchial arteries continue to perfuse and oxygenate that segment, so the tissue survives while the embolus is lysed. In chronic left heart failure, however, sustained pulmonary venous hypertension raises the downstream pressure that bronchial arterial flow must overcome, and the septa are thickened and fibrotic, so bronchial perfusion becomes inadequate. The same small embolus now kills the tissue — and because blood continues to seep in from the bronchial circulation into damaged, leaky capillaries, the infarct is red (haemorrhagic), producing haemoptysis and, through the overlying fibrinous pleural exudate, pleuritic pain. E Antithrombin levels do not vary by pulmonary status. A Embolus size is unrelated to the health of the lung. B Congested lungs have impaired gas exchange and lower oxygen tension. C Alveolar regeneration has no role over this timescale. See §1.5, §4.2, §5.3.

6.4 Glossary of Key Terms

Arranged alphabetically for fast look-up. Each entry is written to stand alone, so it can be read the night before without re-reading the chapter.

Anasarca
Severe, generalised edema with profound subcutaneous swelling; implies advanced cardiac, hepatic or renal failure.
Anaemic infarct
Older term for a white or pale infarct — arterial occlusion in a compact, end-arterial organ.
Antithrombin III
Plasma protease inhibitor that inactivates thrombin and factors IXa, Xa, XIa and XIIa; accelerated about 1000-fold by heparin. Its deficiency causes apparent heparin resistance.
Ascites
Pathological accumulation of fluid in the peritoneal cavity (hydroperitoneum). Commonest cause is cirrhosis with portal hypertension and hypoalbuminaemia.
Bends, the
Acute decompression sickness: severe joint and muscle pain caused by nitrogen bubbles in periarticular tissue.
Brown induration of the lung
Chronic pulmonary congestion: brown from haemosiderin in heart failure cells, indurated from septal fibrosis. Lesion of chronic left heart failure.
Caisson disease
Chronic decompression sickness with avascular (ischaemic) necrosis of bone, typically the femoral and humeral heads.
Cardiac cirrhosis
True fibrous scarring of the liver resulting from very long-standing passive congestion; the end-stage of repeated centrilobular necrosis.
Coagulative necrosis
Necrosis in which cellular outlines are preserved as anucleate "ghosts", because ischaemia denatures both structural proteins and lysosomal enzymes. The pattern of infarcts in every organ except the brain.
Congestion
Passive increase in local blood volume from impaired venous outflow. Blue, cool, swollen, harmful; may be local or systemic.
Cor pulmonale
Right ventricular hypertrophy and eventual failure caused by pulmonary hypertension. Acute cor pulmonale is the abrupt right heart strain of a massive pulmonary embolus.
D-dimer
A fibrin degradation product released when cross-linked fibrin is digested by plasmin; a sensitive but non-specific marker of active clot breakdown.
Diapedesis
Escape of red cells one at a time between endothelial cells whose junctions have been loosened by severe congestion, hypoxia or inflammation — haemorrhage without a visible breach.
DIC
Disseminated intravascular coagulation: widespread microvascular fibrin thrombosis that consumes platelets and clotting factors, producing simultaneous organ ischaemia and bleeding. Also called consumption coagulopathy.
Ecchymosis
Subcutaneous haemorrhage larger than 1–2 cm; a bruise. Its colour sequence dates the injury.
Edema
Increased fluid in the interstitial tissue spaces or body cavities — an extravascular accumulation of the fluid phase of blood.
Elephantiasis
Massive chronic lymphedema with thickened, verrucous skin, classically from lymphatic obstruction by Wuchereria bancrofti (filariasis).
Embolus
Any detached intravascular solid, liquid or gaseous mass carried by the blood to a site distant from its origin. Over 99% are dislodged thrombi.
Exudate
Protein-rich, cellular, high-specific-gravity fluid produced by increased vascular permeability in inflammation; clots on standing.
Factor V Leiden
Point mutation rendering factor Va resistant to cleavage by activated protein C; the commonest inherited thrombophilia.
Fat embolism syndrome
Respiratory distress, neurological change and a petechial rash 24–72 h after long-bone fracture; caused by mechanical obstruction plus free fatty acid toxicity.
Gangrene
Clinical term for necrosis of a large mass of tissue. Dry = arterial occlusion with intact venous drainage; wet = venous obstruction plus bacterial infection; gas = clostridial infection of necrotic muscle.
Haemarthrosis
Bleeding into a joint cavity; hallmark of haemophilia and of secondary hemostasis failure generally.
Haematoma
Accumulation of blood within a tissue, forming a discrete mass. Its significance depends entirely on site.
Haemorrhage
Extravasation of blood from the cardiovascular system. Outcome depends on volume, rate and site.
Haemosiderin
Coarse, golden-brown, iron-containing pigment formed when ferritin aggregates after haemoglobin breakdown; Prussian-blue positive. Iron cannot be excreted, so haemosiderin is the last pigment to disappear.
Heart failure cells
Haemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages (siderophages) found in the alveoli in chronic pulmonary congestion; the cause of rust-coloured sputum.
Hemostasis
The physiological sequence — vasoconstriction, platelet plug, fibrin deposition, clot stabilisation — that stops bleeding at a site of vascular injury.
Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia
IgG against heparin–PF4 complexes activates platelets, causing a falling platelet count together with thrombosis, not bleeding.
Hyperemia
Active increase in local blood volume from arteriolar dilation. Red, warm, beneficial, always local.
Hydropericardium / hydrothorax
Non-inflammatory fluid in the pericardial / pleural cavity.
Infarct
Area of ischaemic necrosis caused by occlusion of arterial supply or venous drainage; characteristically wedge-shaped with the apex at the occluded vessel.
Lines of Zahn
Alternating pale (platelet/fibrin) and dark (red cell) laminations within a thrombus; they prove it formed in flowing blood before death.
Liquefactive necrosis
Necrosis in which the tissue is digested to a fluid-filled cavity. Characteristic of the brain and of abscesses.
Lymphedema
Protein-rich edema from failed lymphatic drainage; becomes firm and non-pitting because the retained protein raises interstitial oncotic pressure and stimulates fibrosis.
Marantic endocarditis
Sterile, non-destructive fibrin–platelet vegetations on the line of valve closure, associated with advanced malignancy and DIC.
Mural thrombus
Thrombus adherent to one wall of a cardiac chamber or the aorta, leaving the lumen partly patent; a common source of systemic emboli.
Mycotic aneurysm
Localised arterial dilation caused by infective destruction of the wall, typically by a septic embolus from endocarditis; prone to fatal rupture.
Nutmeg liver
Chronic passive hepatic congestion: dark red congested and necrotic centrilobular zones alternating with pale, often fatty, periportal parenchyma. Lesion of right heart failure.
Paradoxical embolism
A venous embolus that crosses a right-to-left cardiac shunt and enters the systemic circulation, bypassing the pulmonary filter.
Peau d'orange
Dimpled, orange-peel appearance of skin over dermal lymphatic obstruction — the skin is edematous but tethered at hair follicles. Classic in inflammatory breast carcinoma.
Petechia
Pinpoint 1–2 mm haemorrhage into skin, mucosa or serosa; does not blanch on pressure.
Phlebolith
A calcified organized venous thrombus; commonly an incidental radiological finding in the pelvis.
Primary hemostasis
Formation of the platelet plug by adhesion (vWF–GpIb), activation and aggregation (fibrinogen–GpIIb/IIIa). Its failure causes mucocutaneous bleeding.
Protein C / protein S
Vitamin K–dependent anticoagulants; activated protein C, with protein S as cofactor, inactivates factors Va and VIIIa.
Purpura
Haemorrhage 3–5 mm (up to ~1 cm) into skin or mucosa. Palpable purpura implies vasculitis; flat purpura implies a platelet defect.
Recanalization
Formation of new endothelial-lined channels through an organized thrombus, partly restoring flow but never to normal.
Red (haemorrhagic) infarct
Infarct that remains full of blood: caused by venous occlusion, or arterial occlusion in a loose, spongy, dual-supply organ (lung, bowel, testis, ovary), or after reperfusion.
Rhexis, haemorrhage per
Haemorrhage through a frank structural breach of the vessel wall, as opposed to diapedesis.
Saddle embolus
A large thromboembolus straddling the bifurcation of the main pulmonary artery; usually immediately fatal.
Schistocyte
A fragmented red cell, sheared as it passes through intravascular fibrin strands; the blood-film marker of microangiopathic haemolysis in DIC and TTP.
Secondary hemostasis
Deposition of fibrin by the coagulation cascade, reinforcing the platelet plug. Its failure causes deep and delayed bleeding.
Septic infarct
An infarct seeded by organisms — usually from an infected embolus — which is converted into an abscess because necrotic tissue has no blood supply to deliver defences.
Stasis
Slowing or cessation of blood flow; the dominant flow abnormality in venous thrombosis.
Starling forces
The four pressures governing fluid movement across a capillary: capillary hydrostatic (out), plasma oncotic (in), interstitial hydrostatic (in) and interstitial oncotic (out).
Thrombomodulin
Endothelial receptor that binds thrombin and converts it into an activator of protein C — turning the body's most powerful procoagulant into an anticoagulant.
Thrombosis
Formation of an adherent clotted mass of blood within the cardiovascular system of a living body.
Transudate
Protein-poor, low-specific-gravity, acellular fluid produced by a Starling-force imbalance across an intact vessel wall; does not clot.
Trousseau syndrome
Migratory thrombophlebitis caused by procoagulants released from a tumour; may be the first sign of an occult visceral cancer.
Vegetation
A thrombotic mass on a heart valve. Infective (destructive, contains organisms), marantic (sterile, malignancy) or Libman–Sacks (sterile, SLE, on both leaflet surfaces).
Virchow's triad
The three predisposing influences to thrombosis: endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow (stasis or turbulence), and hypercoagulability.
von Willebrand factor
Plasma multimer that bridges subendothelial collagen to platelet GpIb, enabling adhesion under high shear; also the carrier protein for factor VIII.
White (pale) infarct
Infarct that loses its blood: arterial occlusion in a solid, compact, end-arterial organ (kidney, spleen, heart). Sharply demarcated and yellow-white.
